A purchase that can be revoked is not a purchase. When a streaming service loses a licence, the film you paid for vanishes out of your library, the money stays exactly where it went, and the company that took it will explain, patiently, that nothing has gone wrong, because what you bought was never the film. You bought a permission. The permission ran on a term that was never shown to anybody, and the term is up.

The mechanism is dull, which is how it survives. A rights-holder licenses a title to a service for a fixed number of years; the years run out; the rights-holder wants more money, or wants the title for the service it has just launched itself; and the row of thumbnails under the heading that says "Purchases" is one shorter on Tuesday than it was on Monday. Nobody is notified. There is no refund, because in the store's own account of what happened there was nothing to refund.

This is where the word-magic happens. The buy-button says BUY. The receipt says "purchase". The page nobody opens says "licence", and the licence says the licence can end, which means the store sold two contradictory things and gets to choose which one was real later, when it matters. I've shopped in a lot of shops. None of the others reserved the right to come round and take the couch back because the manufacturer and the retailer had fallen out.

You will hear that this is rare. It isn't rare, it's scheduled: every title in every library on every service sits on a contract with an end date, and the only reason the collection looks permanent is that most of those dates haven't arrived yet. It is a Cheshire Cat library, thumbnails going one at a time, receipts left grinning on the shelf. When Sony told PlayStation owners in Germany and Austria that titles they had purchased were coming off their consoles over a rights issue, the notice was polite, well in advance, and completely beside the point.

If you had a shelf, none of this could happen. A cloud (and you have one, because the shelf was discontinued and nobody asked) does it to everybody who bought the same thing on the same afternoon, at the same instant, without anyone driving anywhere or carrying anything. Deletion used to cost money, and now it costs an API call, which is a change in the price of taking things away from people.

The galling part is that the fix exists and is illegal. A copy on a drive in a drawer cannot be recalled by anyone, and the tools that turn a stream into a drawer-copy are trivial – people write them in an afternoon – but section 1201 of the DMCA makes breaking the lock an offence whether or not you own what is behind it. This is a law that protects the lock and not the buyer. That is a choice somebody made, and it is a fucking indefensible one when the lock's only function is to let a sold thing be sold again.

But the industry's own vocabulary gives the game away. They do not sell copies, they license access, and a licensor is a landlord. You are a tenant in a library you paid to furnish – the rents are the point, the term is the point, and the improvements belong to the estate. I don't think "feudal" is too strong a word, and I have watched people flinch at it while paying a monthly fee to keep hold of their own wedding photographs.

Meanwhile the advice on offer is small and a little humiliating. Keep the disc, buy where they hand over a plain unlocked file, and put that file on a drive with your own hands. Stop calling it a purchase in your head, whatever the button calls it. That is not a solution, it's a coping strategy, and the fact that we learned it quietly, one thinned-out library at a time, is most of the reason it keeps working on us.

We keep being told that ownership was a phase, a quirk of the years when culture arrived in objects, and that access is better because it's bigger. Access is bigger. But it can be swung toward you or away from you at somebody else's discretion, at no cost to them, and the money in their pocket is money handed over in a transaction you believed was finished.

If a store can revoke, it should have to say so on the button, in the same size type as the price, and should have to hand the money back when it does, at the price paid, without a form and without a coupon. None of that is technically hard. It will not happen unprompted, because the party that would have to build it is the party currently holding what we paid.

So the next time a library thins out and a support account explains that rights are complicated, notice the part that is not complicated. The rights-holder wants the title back so it can be sold again. The service wants the word "purchase" to mean whatever it needs to mean on the day somebody asks. Neither of them wants you to own anything, because a thing you own is a thing they can't sell you twice.
