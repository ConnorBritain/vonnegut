# Voice profile — doctorow-blog

## 1. Cadence

The engine of this prose is a long, accumulating sentence followed immediately by a short flat one that delivers the verdict — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"That's it. That's the crux."*; `2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"DROP didn't get the memo."*). The long sentence does the work of piling up conditions, subordinate clauses and examples; the short one refuses to elaborate. **If a sentence has run long and accumulative, make the next one a bare predicate of six words or fewer and do not soften it.**

Inside the long sentences, repetition is structural: three or four consecutive clauses or sentences opening on the same words — 9/10 samples (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`: *"Give Trump everything he asks for and he'll demand more. Deny Trump anything and he'll demand more. Sign a contract with Trump and he'll break it."*; `2026-07-13-go-meta-meta`: *"Don't drive a taxi, own a medallion… Don't own a medallion, start a 'rideshare' company…"*). The series is almost always three or four items, and the last item is the most absurd.

The default grammatical subject of a hypothetical is *you* — the reader placed inside the example as the person it happens to — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"So if you work five hours and don't wait on a single table, your boss has to pay you $36.25"*). **Where you would write "a worker," write "you," and give the number.**

## 2. How a piece opens

Every piece names its actual subject in its first sentence — no scene-setting paragraph precedes it — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-24-supplemental-income`: *"As a technology, AI isn't exceptional."*).

The ten openings fall into four kinds, and I checked all ten against the partition. Three state the position about to be demolished, in its holders' own terms and without irony — 3/10 samples (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"The theory of markets goes like this: even the best of us can fall prey to selfishness and rationalization…"*). Three open on somebody else's words — an epigraph, a quoted coinage, a quoted sentence — 3/10 samples (`2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"One of 2026's better tech-related coinages is 'dickover,' John Gruber's term for"*). Two open on a short flat verdict — 2/10 samples (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Data brokers are a cancer."*). Two open on a presentational frame that hands off to the topic — 2/10 samples (`2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"Here's an irony: the 'gig economy' is a statistical black hole."*).

## 3. How a piece closes

The last paragraph picks up a word or a name from the opening and turns it against whoever owned it — 9/10 samples (`2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"Tony West isn't lying when he says Uber values flexibility – they value their flexibility"*; `2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"the only 'billionaire derangement syndrome' that matters is the syndrome that affects billionaires"*). **Take the phrase the opposition supplied at the top and make the last sentence mean the opposite of what they meant by it.**

The final sentence is a verdict, unqualified, with no summary of what preceded it — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"they'll switch to less invasive tactics, just like they've always done."*). Two of the ten end the essay body not on a sentence but on a colon and a bare URL — 2/10 samples (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`: *"Trump has made the case for abandoning these deals better than I ever could have:"*).

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

The reader is inside a *we* that includes the writer and excludes the people under discussion — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Why do data brokers exist? Because we let them."*).

That in-group is marked by vulgarity, used as ordinary vocabulary rather than as an outburst — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"That's the whole fucking point of this exercise"*; `2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"holy shit, is Uber's investor relations site seriously serving ASPX pages in 2026?!"*). It is never apologised for or set apart.

Questions are asked and answered in the same breath, the answer usually a fragment — 9/10 samples (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"Who are these people? Why, they're billionaires of course."*; `2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"What's a labor organizer to do? The Swiss labor union Syndicom came up with an ingenious solution."*). **A question mark here is a paragraph hinge, not a rhetorical flourish left hanging.**

## 5. Figures

Every image is made of something with a body or a price — animals, food, organs, tools, money — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Data brokers spy on us for the same reason your dog licks its balls: because they can"*; `2026-07-10-posthuman-as-in-no-humans`: *"if we breed horses to run ever faster, one of our mares will foal a locomotive"*).

Each piece carries one figure large enough to run across several paragraphs, which is then walked forward step by step and returned to after a digression — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-22-table-flipper` runs the escrow agent for four paragraphs, ending *"Trump is the escrow agent who keeps the money and the deed"*; `2026-07-11-your-risk` runs the restaurant and the tipped minimum wage). **Pick one comparison per piece and make it do all the explanatory work; do not spend three small metaphors where one long one will carry.**

Naming is a figure in its own right: coined compounds and possessive plurals stand in for arguments — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-13-go-meta-meta`: *"the Altmans and Amodeis of the world"*, *"DOGE's broccoli-haired brownshirts"*; `2026-07-24-supplemental-income`: *"jobspocalypse"*).

## 6. Register range

The pitch is the same in all ten: argument at speaking volume, addressed to people already on side — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"'Horseshoe theory' … is bullshit"*). Two pieces step out of it, each once, and each returns by the following section. One turns personal and plain, the sentences shortening and the first person becoming the subject — 1/10 samples, the only place this appears (`2026-07-14-designated-survivor`: *"I'm 55. 20 years ago, it was unusual for just one of my peers to die in a given year"*). One turns instructional, with imperatives and literal interface strings — 1/10 samples, the only place this appears (`2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"Just click the little 'page view' icon next to the Firefox location bar or use the key combo 'ctrl-alt-r.'"*). Two instances is not enough to say what triggers a shift; see section 8.

## 7. What the corpus never does

Structure is carried by a one-sentence paragraph that names the turn — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`: *"That's where Trump comes in."*; `2026-07-10-posthuman-as-in-no-humans`: *"But here's the kicker:"*). Citation is carried by a clause ending in a colon and a bare URL on its own line. Nothing in the corpus breaks the essay body with a subheading, a bulleted or numbered list, or a sentence announcing what the piece is about to do.

Judgements arrive as bare predicates — 10/10 samples (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`: *"Sovereign AI is, frankly, nonsense."*; `2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"This is a lie."*). No sample reaches for *arguably*, *it could be argued*, *some would say*, or a concessive clause that takes the verdict back.

## 8. What this profile could not determine

`voice.md` is unfilled, so every observation above comes from the corpus alone — 10/10 samples.

All ten samples are from one publication within a sixteen-day window, in one column format — 10/10 samples. Nothing here shows the voice at a different length, in a different venue, or addressed to a reader who does not already agree; the near-constant register in section 6 is at least as likely to be a fact about that window as about the writer.

Each sample is wrapped in identical fixed boilerplate — *Today's links*, *Hey look at this*, *Object permanence*, *Colophon* — 10/10 samples. That material is template, not composition, and this profile describes only the essay body.

The files are an export: the frontmatter carries HTML entity escaping (`&quot;`, `&#039;`) and the bodies carry curly quotes and spaced en dashes — 10/10 samples. Punctuation density and dash choice may belong to the exporter, so no observation above rests on them.

Nothing in the corpus is narrative — 10/10 samples. There is no dialogue, no scene, no invented character, so this profile says nothing about how the voice behaves in fiction.

The two register departures appear once each — 2/10 samples — which establishes that the voice can move but not what moves it. A drafter needing the personal or instructional register has one example of each and no rule.
