Every password manager you've ever loved has the same lifecycle. A couple of cryptographers who actually care ship a thing that does one job — hold your secrets so that even they can't read them — and they say so loudly, in a whitepaper, with math in it. The product gets good. The product gets popular. The product gets acquired. Some private equity shop with a name like a Connecticut law firm writes a check, and the whitepaper stays on the website, and the math in it stays true, and the company that publishes it is now a completely different animal wearing the old one's skin.

That's the trick. The promise doesn't get broken. It gets sold.

Here's the thing about a security promise: it isn't a property of the software. It's a property of the people running the software, which means it lasts exactly as long as their incentives do. Zero-knowledge encryption is a fact about your vault. "We will never build a feature that quietly exfiltrates your vault" is not a fact about anything — it's a promise from a management team that no longer works there. LastPass's founders didn't decide to get breached twice and lie about the scope. That decision was made several owners downstream by people who had never met you.

Think of it as a landlord problem. You signed a lease with a guy who lived in the building, fixed the boiler himself, and knew your kids' names. Then he sold to a REIT. Nothing in the lease changed. The boiler is the same boiler. But the guy who decides whether to fix the boiler now has a spreadsheet where "boiler" is a line item and your continued tenancy is a variable he can solve for. The lease was never the thing keeping you warm. He was.

So the boiler stops getting fixed. Not dramatically — nobody sends a memo saying we've stopped fixing boilers. The security team's headcount doesn't get cut, it just doesn't grow while the customer base triples. The bug bounty stays at the same dollar amount it was in 2019. The rearchitecture that senior engineers have been asking for since the last acquisition doesn't get scheduled, because rearchitecture is eighteen months of no new features, and the sponsor needs a story about growth for the next sale, and the next sale is in thirty-one months. Every one of those is a defensible decision. Together they're the boiler.

And then come the features. Private equity doesn't buy a password manager to run a password manager. It buys a subscription base with the highest switching costs in consumer software — because moving your vault means touching four hundred logins on a Sunday and finding out which of them still work — and then it goes looking for things to sell that base. Family plans. Dark-web monitoring, which is a product that tells you your data was stolen years ago by someone else. Identity theft insurance underwritten by a company that will litigate the claim. A password health dashboard. An AI assistant, obviously. Each one wants a little more access to the thing that was supposed to be inaccessible, and each one ships with a blog post about how it preserves the security model, and the blog post is even mostly true, and the attack surface goes up anyway.

Why do we keep ending up here? Because we let them. Because we treated "the encryption is sound" as the whole question when the question was always "who will own this in five years and what will they need from it." We audited the cryptography and skipped the cap table.

The answer isn't a better whitepaper. It's ownership structures that can't be sold out from under you: local-first vaults in a format you can export at 2AM without permission, open implementations where the client is auditable and forkable, standards that let you leave with your data instead of having to rebuild it by hand. Everything that makes a vault easy to leave makes it harder to loot. That's the whole fucking point. A password manager you can walk away from in an afternoon is one whose owners have to keep earning you.

The founders who wrote that whitepaper weren't lying. They meant every word of it, and the math still checks out. They just weren't in a position to promise the only thing you actually needed promised: that the people holding your secrets next year would be the kind of people you'd have handed them to. Nobody can promise that. They can only make it not matter — and the ones who won't are telling you exactly what they plan to do with it.
