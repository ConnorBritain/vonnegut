Ownership that ends when the maker presses a button isn't ownership; it's a rental with better packaging. If you buy a device, you should control whether its maker can disable features after sale. That's the whole argument, and we can make it more complicated only by pretending the obvious distinction isn't there.

When you purchase something, you expect to decide how it behaves within the ordinary limits of using it safely and lawfully. You might accept updates, repairs, restrictions, or a warranty that expires (those are terms about the thing you bought). But a feature that works today and vanishes tomorrow because the maker changed its mind is a different arrangement. Who owns the device then? You do, in the thin ceremonial sense; the maker does, whenever control matters.

Of course, no one is saying that a maker must preserve every feature forever under every circumstance. A feature might be unsafe, defective, or incompatible with something else, and we should be able to discuss those cases without handing over the entire principle. The question is who gets the final say after the sale: you, who paid for the device, or the maker, who wants to retain a remote hand on the controls? Those aren't equivalent powers.

Think of the device as a small house with a control panel on the wall. You bought the house, moved your stuff in, and arranged the rooms around what the panel does. Then the builder keeps a key and announces that one room is closed because the builder has decided the room's future is more convenient elsewhere. You'd call that a remarkable property dispute, not a routine maintenance decision. The remote hand has turned your purchase into an escrow arrangement, with the thing you paid for held hostage behind a menu.

I know the reply: the maker created the device, so the maker should be allowed to decide what it does. Sure, creation matters when we're talking about the maker's own tools, designs, and services. It doesn't follow that creation grants permanent authority over an object after someone else has bought it. If I make a chair and sell it to you, I don't retain the right to remove a leg because I suddenly prefer stools. That would be bullshit, and adding software doesn't make the underlying claim magically respectable.

Technically curious readers will notice that software makes the boundary less visible. A feature may be implemented in code, connected to a service, or exposed through an account (the implementation can be complicated). But complexity doesn't dissolve the user's interest in control; it merely gives the maker more ways to exercise power without touching the physical object. We should be suspicious when an engineering detail is used as a moral escape hatch. The question isn't whether the maker can technically disable the feature. The question is whether it should be entitled to do so after the sale.

There are practical reasons to care about this besides the insult. When a feature can disappear, your plans inherit a risk you didn't choose. You may have learned a workflow, built a habit, or depended on a device's behavior, only to discover that the product includes a silent trapdoor. The maker has shifted the risk onto you while keeping the power to change the bargain. That's not a technicality; it's a damn bad deal.

We don't need to ban improvement, updates, or honest change. We need a clear rule: buying a device should buy meaningful control over the features represented as part of that device. If a maker wants continuing authority, it should say so plainly before the sale, and you should be able to reject that arrangement without losing the basic use of what you bought. Otherwise, the product is a subscription wearing a plastic costume. And if we let every disappearing feature pass as ordinary, we'll teach makers that ownership is something they can revoke whenever it suits them. That isn't ownership. It's a permission slip, and you paid for it.
