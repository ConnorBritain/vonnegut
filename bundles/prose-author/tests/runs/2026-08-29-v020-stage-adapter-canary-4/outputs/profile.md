## 1. Cadence

**Contraction and negation.** Contractions keep exposition conversational and give polemical claims a spoken, quick-moving cadence. They are especially effective in transitions, direct address, dismissals, and compressed conclusions, where formal expansion would sound ceremonious. Prefer the contracted form in ordinary argument and explanation; reserve a fuller form for deliberate emphasis.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

**Person, number, and reader stance.** Direct second-person address makes the reader a participant in the argument rather than a distant observer. It commonly appears when explaining a system, posing a practical consequence, or turning a general claim into an immediate test: “If you think…” Use you to make abstract stakes feel personal and actionable.

## 5. Figures

**Figures and analogy vocabulary.** Figures translate institutional or technical mechanics into vivid bodily, domestic, commercial, or fantastical scenes: a system may “shift risk,” “punch yourself in the face,” or become an escrow agent. Comparisons are often extended through several concrete consequences before returning to the thesis. Use one governing analogy to make structure memorable.

## 6. Register range

**Profanity and vulgarity.** Vulgar language acts as argumentative punctuation: it punctures euphemism, marks contempt for a dishonest premise, or supplies comic force to a concrete example. It often lands after a technical or institutional description, creating an abrupt register drop. Use it selectively at the sharpest turn, not as continuous decoration.

**Self-reference and biography.** First-person singular reference presents the writer as an involved arguer, witness, or example rather than as the subject of memoir. It introduces a remembered encounter, personal judgment, practical recommendation, or limited disclosure before returning to the public claim. Use I to ground an argument briefly, then widen its scope.

**Qualification and hedging.** Qualification appears as explicit concessions, scenario limits, and brief admissions before the argument resumes its force. The prose acknowledges exceptions—“Of course” and “Sure” often introduce them—then distinguishes the exception from the governing pattern. Place a concession immediately before the sharper claim it narrows or strengthens.

**Named opponents, allies, and sources.** Sources enter as named people, institutions, linked articles, quoted formulations, or recurring interlocutors. Their claims are summarized, credited, extended, and sometimes corrected: “there’s one area where I think Quiggin’s got it wrong.” Introduce evidence plainly, then state exactly what the source establishes and where your argument departs.
