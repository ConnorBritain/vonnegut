# Voice profile — doctorow-blog

Ten blog posts, 17,549 body words, all published under the author's own name on his own site with no editor between him and the page. Everything below is measured over those ten bodies (the essay between the first `(permalink)` line and the next section marker); link lines are part of the body and are included in the word total, which matters for the rates.

## 1. How the sentences run

The default sentence is long and accumulative — a claim, then the clauses that earn it, stacked with commas and dashes until the thing is fully paid for. Across 585 sentences the mean is 29.8 words, and **113 sentences run 45 words or longer — 10/10 samples, several times per piece** (`2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"It's the boss, not the server, who decides where the restaurant will be, which hours it will keep, which food it will serve, how much the food costs, what advertisements to run, and where and when to run them."*).

What stops them is a very short flat one. **55 sentences of seven words or fewer — 10/10 samples, several times per piece** (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"That's it. That's the crux."*; `2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"It's bullshit."*). If a sentence has just run forty words explaining how something works, make the next one under seven words and make it the verdict.

The colon is the hinge of the whole style: **305 of them — 10/10 samples, throughout** (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"They should understand that capitalists hate capitalism…"* follows a colon; `2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Here's the thing:"*). It does two jobs — announce-then-deliver inside a sentence, and claim-then-evidence at the end of a paragraph.

## 2. How a piece opens

Three shapes, no composite. **Three of ten open on a flat verdict of sixteen words or fewer, subject named, judgement delivered — 3/10 samples** (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Data brokers are a cancer."*; `2026-07-24-supplemental-income`: *"As a technology, AI isn't exceptional."*).

**Four of ten hand the floor to someone else first — 4/10 samples** — either a pointer sentence (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`: *"Here's a sentence that stopped me in my tracks last week:"*; `2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"Here's an irony:"*) or a straight epigraph or borrowed definition before any argument (`2026-07-13-go-meta-meta` opens on the Rubáiyát; `2026-07-21-dickovers` opens on Gruber's definition of *dickover*).

**Three of ten state the received wisdom at length before demolishing it — 3/10 samples** (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"The theory of markets goes like this: even the best of us can fall prey to selfishness and rationalization…"*).

## 3. How a piece closes

**The final paragraph reuses a phrase the piece itself introduced, turned around — 10/10 samples** (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices` ends on *"the only 'billionaire derangement syndrome' that matters is the syndrome that affects billionaires"*; `2026-07-11-your-risk` on *"they value their flexibility"*). Whatever term the piece has been contesting is the last thing the reader sees.

**The close is a declarative, not an exhortation — 9/10 samples** (`2026-07-14-designated-survivor`: *"The fact that our political classes seem to think that they can choose the time and manner of their passing is baffling."*). The exception closes on a rhetorical question instead — 1/10 samples, `2026-07-13-go-meta-meta`.

## 4. Who is being addressed

The reader is *you*, and *you* is the person things are done to. **395 second-person words — 10/10 samples, throughout, 22.51 per 1,000 words** (`2026-07-11-your-risk`: *"If your boss overestimates the amount of business, much of the cost of that miscalculation comes out of your paycheck."*). Note the construction: not "workers' paycheques" but *your* paycheque.

Alongside it, a *we* that includes the reader in a shared polity, never an authorial *we*: **169 instances of we/us/our — 10/10 samples, throughout, 9.63 per 1,000** (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"because they can, and we don't stop them"*).

Contraction is near-total: **427 contractions against 9 uncontracted negations — 10/10 samples, throughout, 24.33 per 1,000**. Write *isn't*, *won't*, *there's*.

Claims are unhedged. **Only 19 hedge markers in the whole corpus — 9/10 samples, once or twice per piece, 1.08 per 1,000** (`2026-07-24-supplemental-income`: *"I think billionaire solipsism can account for much…but I don't think it's the whole story"*). The support comes after the claim, not before it, and it comes as a link.

Profanity is a verdict marker, not decoration. **21 instances — 10/10 samples, once or twice per piece, 1.20 per 1,000** (`2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"It's bullshit."*; `2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"you can just give them away and tell the cartel to fuck off"*). It arrives at the point of maximum contempt and then the sentence stops.

The opposition is handled by quoting it. **154 short quoted terms of four words or fewer — 10/10 samples, throughout, 8.78 per 1,000** (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"consumer welfare"*, *"revealed preferences"*, *"regulatory capture"*, *"meritocracy"*). Named people take the blame beside the abstractions — Tony West, Thiel, Musk, Carney — so the epithets land on somebody.

## 5. Figures

The characteristic figure is an extended analogy borrowed from an ordinary transaction and then run for several paragraphs before being brought back — **9/10 samples**: the escrow agent standing in for the US as trusted third party (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`), the restaurateur and the tipped minimum wage standing in for Uber (`2026-07-11-your-risk`), the Puritan doctrine of divine election standing in for meritocracy (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`). Build the analogy on something with a counter and a till, not on something abstract.

Under that, the body supplies the insults. **19 body-and-orifice words — 8/10 samples, once or twice per piece, 1.08 per 1,000** (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`: *"they emerged from a lucky orifice"*; `2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"for the same reason your dog licks its balls"*). And the second source is borrowed narrative — scripture, myth, pop culture — **7/10 samples** (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"a locked filing cabinet stuck in a disused lavatory with a sign on the door saying 'Beware of the Leopard'"*; `2026-07-10-posthuman-as-in-no-humans`: *"one of our mares will foal a locomotive"*).

## 6. What shifts

The first person is always present — **110 instances of I/me/my — 10/10 samples, several times per piece, 6.27 per 1,000** — but the writer appears as a person with a history in only half of them (`2026-07-14-designated-survivor`: *"I'm 55… my doctor just informed me that I am cancer free"*), and as an arguer in the rest. When the subject is his own mortality or his own trade, the links thin out and the sentences shorten.

The other shift is instructional. **20 sentences led by an imperative — 7/10 samples, once or twice per piece, 1.14 per 1,000**, concentrated where the piece turns to what the reader can actually do (`2026-07-21-dickovers`: *"Just click the 'Kill Sticky' bookmarklet and all the static elements in the current tab go away."*). In that mode the sentences get short and procedural and the second person becomes literal rather than rhetorical.

## 7. What the corpus never does

Structure is carried by paragraph and citation: a claim ends on a colon, and the evidence is a bare URL alone on the next line. **103 claim-colon-link units — 10/10 samples, several times per piece, 5.87 per 1,000** (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Congress hasn't passed a new federal consumer privacy law since 1988…"* then the link). Nothing in the corpus breaks a piece with a markdown heading, a bulleted list, or a numbered list — 10/10 samples, zero instances of each.

The joints between clauses are dashes and colons. **73 dashes — 10/10 samples, several times per piece, 4.16 per 1,000** (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"it's about whether we are ruled at all, or whether we are free"* is preceded by a dashed turn). Against that, the semicolon appears 12 times in the whole corpus and in only 5/10 samples, and where it does it is separating items in a list rather than balancing two clauses.

## 8. What this could not be determined

`voice.md` is unfilled, so every observation above is derived from the corpus alone and none of it is corroborated by the author's own account — 10/10 samples.

All ten samples are consecutive posts from one blog across sixteen days, all argumentative essays on technology and political economy, all between 898 and 2,559 body words. Nothing here shows how the voice behaves in fiction, in a two-line link blurb, in a piece addressed to a hostile correspondent rather than a sympathetic readership, or at a length far from that band — 10/10 samples.

The samples are web scrapes, and link lines were retained in the body. Every rate above is therefore per 1,000 words *including* those URL lines; against a draft with no links, the same rate will produce slightly more of the habit than the corpus does — 10/10 samples.
