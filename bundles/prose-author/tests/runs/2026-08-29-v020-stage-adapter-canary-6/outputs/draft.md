```markdown
Ownership is the promise that a thing remains yours after the money changes hands. If the maker can reach into that thing later and disable a feature, then you bought a device with a landlord hiding inside it. The landlord might be polite, automated, and represented by a screen that says “service unavailable,” but the arrangement is still bullshit.

You can call this a licensing model, a service relationship, or a product with continuing support. Those labels describe the maker’s interests. They don’t answer the buyer’s question: what, exactly, did your money purchase? If a feature worked when you bought the device and the maker can remove it afterward, your ownership has a hole in it (and the hole is controlled by somebody else).

There are plenty of reasons a maker might want this power. A company might want to change a product’s behavior, respond to a security problem, or end a service that has become expensive. I think some of those reasons are real. They still don’t settle the central issue, because a reasonable explanation for taking control isn’t the same thing as a right to take control.

Consider the ordinary shape of a sale. You inspect the thing, decide what it does, pay for it, and take it home. The maker’s role changes at that point – from seller to somebody who may offer repairs, updates, or new products. If the maker can later decide that one of the device’s functions has expired, then the sale never completed in the ordinary sense. It became a subscription with a large one-time payment and a small-print trapdoor (a feature, a service, a relationship).

What happens to your risk? You’ve paid the price, but we’re left carrying the uncertainty. You can maintain the device carefully and follow every instruction, yet your control depends on a decision made elsewhere. Our physical possession becomes a kind of stage prop: it looks like ownership from across the room, while the meaningful authority lives in a server, an account, or a policy you can’t change.

The usual reply is that you agreed to the terms. Maybe you did. But did you have a meaningful choice? Could you buy the device with the same features and without the remote switch? Could you repair the software, preserve the function, or keep using the thing after the maker loses interest? If the answer is no, then the agreement describes a condition of access, not a genuine transfer of control.

This distinction matters even when the disabled feature seems minor. A company can remove a convenience today and a necessary function tomorrow. You might say that nobody would buy a device under those conditions, but people often learn the condition only after the purchase. The surprise isn’t an accident in the consumer’s understanding; it’s the predictable result of calling a controlled service a product.

We should require makers to say what power they retain, when they can use it, and what happens afterward. More importantly, we should treat durable functionality as part of the thing sold. A device that can be remotely diminished is a different device from the one you examined at the register, even if the casing, battery, and marketing copy look identical.

I’m not arguing that every update must preserve every behavior forever. I’m arguing for a boundary: the maker can improve what you own, but it shouldn’t be able to revoke the substance of the sale. My objection is practical as much as philosophical. When you buy a thing, you should receive control proportionate to the price, the promise, and the ordinary meaning of “buy.”

We can keep pretending that possession is ownership, or we can describe the arrangement honestly. If your device can be switched off piece by piece after sale, then your purchase has handed someone else the keys. Give the keys back.
```
