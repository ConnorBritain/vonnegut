```markdown
# A manual is a tool

A service manual belongs in the toolbox. If a manufacturer sells you a machine but withholds the diagrams, codes, procedures, and fault trees needed to repair it, it has sold you a locked toolbox with one useful drawer. You can see the wrench through the glass; you just can’t reach it. Calling the withheld material “intellectual property” doesn’t alter the transaction (a label isn’t a hinge) – it merely identifies the owner of the lock.

For an independent shop, missing information becomes billable time that nobody can honestly bill. A technician searches, disassembles, tests, guesses, and sometimes sends the job away. The customer loses time; the shop loses capacity; the authorized channel gains leverage. What happens when you must spend two hours discovering a procedure that should take ten minutes? You don’t recover that cost by wishing harder; it comes out of your margin, your schedule, or both.

This isn’t abstract overhead. If you can’t quote a job with confidence, your estimate becomes a wager. If you buy access to one portal, another brand may demand another fee, login, tool, or approval. Every gate turns ordinary repair into a casino game in which the house writes the rules and the small shop stakes its afternoon. You’re paying for permission to discover whether the work can be done.

I’m using “manual” broadly. I don’t mean only a bound book or a downloadable PDF. I mean schematics, diagnostic codes, calibration steps, software tools, connector specifications, and the sequence that makes a replacement part function. My point is practical: information is part of the equipment required for the job. I call it a tool because withholding it produces exactly the same result as removing a socket from the bench midway through a repair.

Documentation and parts pairing are the same lock seen from opposite sides. Pairing connects a component to software approval, a serial record, or a calibration process. A replacement can be physically correct and electrically sound, yet the device can still refuse to use it (or use it badly). If you can’t access the pairing procedure, you don’t truly have access to the part – the part remains a prop. The manual supplies the missing motion between installation and operation.

Who pays for this arrangement? The shop pays in subscriptions, idle bays, training time, duplicated equipment, and work turned away. The customer pays through delay, fewer repair choices, and equipment discarded before its useful life ends. The public pays through wasted material. Describing all of that as protection for intellectual property is bullshit – ownership of information has become control over somebody else’s physical property.

We need a disclosure rule built around the repair, rather than around the preferred format of a manufacturer. Our rule should require every seller of repairable equipment to provide the information, software access, and pairing procedures needed for diagnosis, maintenance, and replacement of parts. Disclosure should arrive when the product is sold, in a usable and searchable form (open documentation is better than a screenshot maze), on fair terms available equally to independent and authorized shops.

The rule also needs teeth. Require stable access for the supported life of the product, prompt publication of revisions, and interfaces that permit an independent technician to complete the same functional steps available inside an authorized network. Trade secrets unrelated to repair can remain protected; safety procedures can carry clear warnings. But a claim of secrecy should identify the specific protected material (rather than swallowing an entire manual) and survive review by a regulator.

Then we can return the transaction to something recognizable. A buyer gets a machine; a repairer gets the tools needed to repair it; a manufacturer gets payment for the product, not a permanent tollbooth beside it. When you own the hardware, we should ensure that the manual opens the toolbox. A manual is a tool, and withholding it takes that tool from all of us.
```
