# Choice screens are not market choice

A choice screen looks like a market in miniature: several logos, a neat list, and a prompt asking you to pick. But the decisive choice happened earlier. Someone selected the candidates, wrote the eligibility rules, ordered the list, and decided which options were too troublesome to appear. I call that a menu, not a market. A restaurant can offer you six desserts without letting you into the kitchen (or even admitting that another kitchen exists). The interface displays variety – the gatekeeper retains authorship of the field.

Defaults matter because attention is scarce and interfaces are work. When a screen opens with one option already marked, that mark isn't neutral. It carries momentum: fewer clicks, less reading, less uncertainty. If you're trying to finish setup before a meeting, you don't conduct a miniature antitrust inquiry; you accept the path that has been paved for you. The default turns placement into distribution (and distribution into revenue). You can still change it, of course. But how many obstacles can be added before “choice” becomes a technically accurate description of a practical trap?

Who chose the paving stones? The same gatekeeper that benefits from steering traffic can define the categories, set the admission price, demand technical integration, and reserve the best position. We should care about each lever because they compound. A rival may be listed yet buried; admitted yet burdened; visible yet unable to communicate directly with customers. Listing criteria can perform the same exclusion more quietly than an outright ban, and with better optics. I don't need to assume a secret conspiracy to see the structure. Control over the ballot is power over the election – especially when the ballot box sits inside a product people already use.

Nominal choice can therefore preserve the very power it claims to dilute. A narrow remedy can rearrange visible options while leaving contracts, fees, ranking systems, data access, and technical permissions untouched. Give you three browsers, payment systems, or app stores, but let one firm decide which three qualify, what warnings surround them, and whether your prior settings survive. You receive alternatives while the selector keeps the choke point. The screen is a pressure valve (enough movement to quiet criticism) attached to the same damn machine. I think technical debates get lost in the number of buttons, as though adding buttons necessarily redistributes authority. It doesn't.

Think of a casino that lets you choose any table while the house chooses the games, writes the odds, owns the chips, and controls the exits. Your freedom among tables is real, but it isn't market power. The house can comply with a rule requiring “choice” while preserving every mechanism that makes the choice profitable to it. We aren't freed merely because the cage has several painted doors (and a cheerful onboarding flow). My point isn't that screens are useless. They can help. It's that interface pluralism can't substitute for structural pluralism.

A meaningful remedy changes who controls entry and whether switching is survivable. It lets qualified providers reach customers under rules the incumbent can't rewrite for private advantage; makes the selection order neutral or genuinely randomized; preserves settings and data when you move; and forbids retaliation against developers who tell customers about other routes. Those details sound procedural. They're the difference between opening a gate and painting an “open” sign on it. We also need auditing, appeal, and enforcement outside the gatekeeper's chain of command – because a right that can be delayed until a rival dies isn't a right.

That doesn't mean every listing decision can vanish. Security, privacy, compatibility, and fraud checks are legitimate constraints, and I wouldn't ask a remedy to pretend otherwise. The question is who writes those checks, who can contest them, and who gains from delay. If we leave those powers with the incumbent, we'll have regulated the presentation while preserving the business model. You may get a prettier picker. Choice screens can display market choice only after law and engineering move control beyond the screen. Until then, the screen isn't a remedy. It's a showroom built by the landlord, stocked by the landlord, and offered as proof that the tenants are free.
