# The server was part of the appliance.

When a vendor announces that its connected-home cloud will close, it often describes the decision as the retirement of an online service (the servers that authenticate, coordinate, and relay commands). For an owner, the hidden consequence is larger: part of the appliance itself is being removed. The thermostat may remain on the wall, the camera may still have a lens, and the hub may keep blinking, but the bargain has changed—the product isn't the product that was sold.

Exactly what breaks depends on the design. Remote controls may disappear. Voice commands (when routed through the vendor) may fail. Cameras may stop recording or sending alerts. Schedules, automations, guest access, energy reports, and integrations with other devices can vanish. Even a button inside the same house may depend on a distant machine—an absurd detour that becomes visible only when that machine goes dark. The hardware doesn't need to be physically broken to become substantially less useful.

Some functions may survive. A bulb might still switch on manually, or a thermostat might hold its last temperature. But partial survival does not preserve the purchase. If the mobile app provided the only controls, losing the app may turn adjustable hardware into a fixed object. If setup requires an account (including setup after a factory reset), a working device may become impossible to reinstall. Ownership can't mean keeping the shell while the vendor withdraws the ordinary means of operating it.

Consider the familiar household scenario. A router is replaced, the device loses its settings, and the setup process asks the cloud to approve a new connection (a certificate, account, or activation token). The cloud is gone. Is the appliance still meaningfully yours? It's a locked cabinet whose key was stored at the vendor's office, except the office has closed and the cabinet is bolted to the house.

The original sale included more than plastic, chips, and a power cord. It included the continuing ability to perform the advertised functions (the continuing service was embedded in the product's operation). A shutdown therefore changes the original bargain after payment. Terms of service may reserve broad discretion, but those terms are not a substitute for a device that performs its basic job. A disclaimer won't make a dead feature work, and it does nothing for an owner who selected one product because of that feature.

Cloud service can provide real benefits. It can make remote access convenient, simplify updates, and connect devices across networks (on a home and away from it). Those advantages don't require designing the appliance as a permanent cloud dependent. That's the crucial distinction: cloud support can add capability without becoming a single point of failure. When every command needs permission from a remote server, a business decision becomes a switch inside the home—one the owner cannot control.

Local operation would protect the core functions. Controls should work across the local network (without contacting the vendor), schedules should run on the device or hub, and recordings should have a local destination. Setup and reset should remain possible after an account system disappears. Published protocols would let compatible software replace a discontinued app, while export tools would preserve settings and data. Security updates may still require outside support, but losing updates is different from losing the basic ability to turn on a light or read a sensor.

A responsible shutdown plan could also release the necessary software (the code or protocol documentation), transfer service to a capable operator, or provide a long transition period. Each option carries costs and security questions. None erases the basic principle. We can accept that companies and products sometimes fail without accepting that purchased appliances must fail with them.

Manufacturers should build local control into connected devices from the beginning, and regulators should not treat cloud dependence as a mere service detail. Required disclosures can reveal reliance before sale, but disclosure alone shouldn't excuse avoidable lock-in. The stronger rule is simple: when the server is essential to an appliance, its operation is part of our bargain, and the owner needs a durable way to keep the appliance working after the cloud closes.
