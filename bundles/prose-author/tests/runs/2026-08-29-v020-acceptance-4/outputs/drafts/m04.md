```markdown
An online age-checking system may arrive wearing the label of a child-safety measure. The law may say that a site must confirm only whether a visitor falls above or below an age threshold (usually eighteen or another statutory line). But the machinery needed to answer that narrow question can create a way to identify, sort, and authorize people across the internet. The label isn't the architecture. A gate built for one room can become the front door for the whole building—especially once institutions discover that the lock exists.

Age checking sounds like a single transaction: prove a fact, enter the site, move on. Yet a system has to decide who asks, what evidence counts, who verifies it (the site, a contractor, or an issuer), and what record remains. If the answer depends on an account, token, credential, or recurring verification, the system doesn't merely check age. It establishes a reusable relationship between a person and an authority. That relationship is identity infrastructure—even if the database stores a category instead of a birth date. The shift happens through incentives, not necessarily through a grand plan. A verifier that can confirm one attribute (age) can be asked to confirm another. A token accepted by one service can become attractive to others. An agency that oversees compliance may seek clearer audit trails (records showing that checks occurred). Each step can sound practical in isolation—cheaper integration, easier enforcement, fewer duplicate systems—but together they produce a common credential layer. The vault becomes more valuable as more doors depend on it, and the consequences of error grow with every new use.

That expansion changes the stakes. What happens when a person lacks the accepted document (a driver's license, passport, or equivalent), when a verifier rejects a legitimate credential, when a shared device exposes an age status, or when a breach links the check to browsing activity? A narrow law may leave these cases outside its frame, but the infrastructure can't. It must make rules about eligibility, correction, retention, access, and appeal. Those rules determine who can speak, read, learn, or participate without passing through an identity gate.

Privacy-preserving design can reduce some risks. A service could return only “over the threshold” (not a name or birth date), separate the verifier from the website, and delete transaction data quickly. Those safeguards matter. They do not, however, settle the institutional question—whether access to ordinary online life will likely become conditioned on carrying an approved credential. Technical minimization limits what one check reveals; it does not prevent the checking mechanism from spreading. A card catalog is safer than a dossier, but a catalog still decides who gets a card. The law's boundaries therefore have to govern the system, not just the first use case. Any data collected for age verification shouldn't be repurposed for advertising, law enforcement fishing expeditions, or unrelated eligibility decisions (the service must remain purpose-limited). Credentials must remain optional where less intrusive evidence would work. Independent audits should test both privacy and exclusion, while deletion rules should cover every participant (including vendors and subcontractors). Enforcement should reach deletion failures and secondary uses, not merely faulty age decisions. A person needs a route to challenge a failure without surrendering identity data. Otherwise the safety measure becomes a dragnet with a polite interface.

Parents and privacy advocates don't have to choose between protecting children and protecting everyone else's ability to use the internet without an identity card. That's a false bargain. We can demand both: effective safety measures and strict limits on any credential system built to enforce them. Lawmakers should not authorize an age check without purpose limits, data deletion, meaningful alternatives, independent oversight, and a ban on expansion into general identity. Ask your representative to put those protections in the law itself. Once we normalize a credential at the entrance to ordinary speech, it won't stay narrow simply because the statute says it is.
```
