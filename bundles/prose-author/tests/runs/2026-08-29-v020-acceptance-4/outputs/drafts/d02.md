```markdown
A shift announced weeks ahead and a shift changed tonight may contain the same hours, the same wage, and the same job. But they are different bargains. When software can rewrite your week after you have arranged your life, the missing item is control – and control is what we bargain over at work. The inconvenience is real, but it isn't the whole injury.

What happens when you can't refuse a last-minute change? You're made to hold the evening open without being paid for the option. Your child care, second job, class, sleep, and bus connection become shock absorbers (the trip is yours, but the timetable belongs to someone else). The system doesn't merely move work; it transfers the cost of uncertainty from the firm to you.

That transfer changes conduct before any shift changes. If you know your hours may be scrambled, then every plan becomes provisional. Invitations are declined. Appointments are postponed. Better work becomes harder to seek because interviews require a dependable gap. The boss has bought an option on your time without paying the option price (the hours outside work remain nominally ours). This isn't flexibility. It is a private claim on public time.

Think of a casino in which the dealer may alter the rules after every bet. The player still appears to choose, but the choice has been hollowed out. Scheduling software can create the same arrangement: management gets information, speed, and discretion; workers get a blinking notification and a countdown. If your refusal carries lost hours or worse assignments, you are playing a game whose rulebook moves whenever the house begins to lose. The software doesn't invent that power. It automates it (a polite interface around an old coercion).

I have no sentimental objection to software. I mean that the machine should be judged by the authority it distributes, not by the smoothness of its screen. I call a tool useful when it reduces drudgery for the people doing the work. I think we should call it bullshit when it reduces friction for management by turning everyone else's life into spare capacity. A clean interface can administer a dirty bargain with extraordinary efficiency.

Of course, some changes are unavoidable. A worker gets sick; equipment fails; demand genuinely surprises. But the existence of emergencies doesn't justify making emergency conditions routine. A fair system would make short notice expensive for the party imposing it – through premium pay, minimum notice, guaranteed hours, and a real right to decline (including protection against quiet retaliation). Then the firm won't treat uncertainty as free, and software can't make the charge disappear.

This is also why individual coping is inadequate. A better calendar or faster notification may soften the collision, but it leaves the steering wheel in the same hands. Workers need records of changes, enforceable notice periods, compensation, and collective control over scheduling rules. When you organise around predictability, the demand asks more than comfort. It asks who owns the hours beyond the shift and who pays when plans are broken. That question binds us together.

Predictability is sometimes described as a benefit, as though it were a nicer chair in the break room. It is closer to a boundary around a human life. Without that boundary, every unscheduled hour becomes inventory waiting to be claimed. We should treat short-notice scheduling as what it is: control exercised through uncertainty, with the costs pushed downward and obedience pulled upward. A stable schedule is more than convenience. It is time returned to its owner.
```
