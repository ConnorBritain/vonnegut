```markdown
Public court records are increasingly reached through two doors: the government’s own electronic system (PACER in the federal courts) and commercial databases that reorganize the same material for paying customers. The stated promise is convenience. The hidden consequence is a two-tier courthouse—one entrance for institutions that can absorb fees, and another for everyone else, who must ration searches through records created and maintained by public authority.

Court records aren’t ordinary commodities. They show how judges interpret law, how prosecutors use power, and how civil disputes are resolved, including the filings that never produce a headline. Access to that material supports scrutiny of institutions, reporting, legal research, and informed participation. A system that technically permits access but makes sustained use unaffordable does not deliver meaningful openness; it delivers a viewing window with a meter attached.

The fees may look modest when listed one page at a time (often described as a charge per page). But serious inquiry rarely begins with a known document. It requires searching, opening false leads, tracing related cases, and checking later entries (motions, orders, exhibits, and amended complaints). A researcher can’t know which path matters before paying to inspect it. That pricing model turns uncertainty into revenue—and makes curiosity itself expensive.

This burden falls unevenly. Large firms and data vendors can spread subscription costs across many clients. Independent journalists, local advocates, small nonprofits, self-represented litigants, and residents (people following a case in their own community) often can’t. What kind of public record is public only after you buy the right search tools? The answer isn’t that every interface must be elaborate. It is that price should not decide who can investigate public power.

Private databases do add value. Better indexing, alerts, citation tools, and bulk analysis can save time, especially across hundreds of dockets. Companies may charge for those enhancements. But the underlying records are not their raw material to monopolize. When the public system is difficult to search or expensive to use, the premium product stops being an optional convenience—it becomes the practical gateway to law.

The distinction matters because access and analysis are different goods. Government should provide reliable, searchable records in open formats (with privacy protections and lawful sealing intact). Vendors can then compete over interfaces, annotation, and specialized research, including tools built for particular professions. That arrangement preserves room for innovation without making a private account the admission ticket to a public institution. Although the baseline won’t erase every access gap, it removes the government-created toll.

Cost is only part of the concern. Court files can contain sensitive details (addresses, medical facts, financial records, or allegations later disputed). Wider access could increase the harm caused by careless republication or automated profiling. That’s a legitimate danger, but scarcity by fee is a crude privacy policy. It shields people with obscurity only until a well-funded actor arrives, and it leaves everyone else outside the vault. Privacy rules should govern sensitive information directly.

A sound public system would pair broad access with narrow, enforceable safeguards (redaction standards, prompt correction procedures, and review of sealed material). It would also offer usable search and no-cost retrieval for core judicial documents (beyond a bare docket index). Rate limits could address genuine technical strain, but they shouldn’t become a disguised paywall. Public administration requires investment—democracy usually does.

We should insist that our courts treat digital access as part of the public courthouse rather than as a side business financed by those trying to observe it. Congress and court administrators should fund modern record systems, eliminate charges for basic access, and publish records in formats that keep the public from being locked into a single vendor or a single brittle portal. Private research services can remain useful, but they must compete above a free public foundation. A right that depends on a costly subscription is a privilege wearing public clothes. Courts should stop dressing it that way.
```
