```markdown
# A manual is a tool.

The right-to-repair debate is often framed as a fight over parts. But for an independent repair shop, the first missing part may be information: service manuals (disassembly instructions), wiring diagrams, diagnostic trees, and calibration procedures. When manufacturers withhold that material as intellectual property, the stated defense of creative work conceals a practical cost—the shop must diagnose a machine while being denied the map. A manual isn't decoration for a product. It is a tool.

Intellectual property can protect original expression without turning every useful fact about a machine into a company secret. A repair manual may contain expressive writing, but its practical core (connector locations, error codes, and test values) describes how the purchased device functions. Treating all of it as an indivisible proprietary work doesn't preserve creativity; it blocks maintenance. A technician can't substitute cleverness for a missing voltage specification—guesswork costs time and can damage equipment.

That cost lands hardest on small shops. A large service network can spread diagnostic subscriptions, specialized equipment, and training expenses across many locations. An independent operation (perhaps two technicians and one lift) has fewer repairs over which to recover those charges. Every hour spent tracing an undocumented circuit is an hour unavailable for paid work. Every uncertain estimate (labor plus the risk of a second teardown) makes a repair harder to offer at a price a customer will accept. Some viable jobs simply won't enter the queue.

Documentation also determines whether a replacement part is genuinely usable. Parts pairing links a component to software authorization (a serial-number check, calibration handshake, or activation step). The physical part may fit perfectly while the device rejects it, limits its functions, or displays a persistent warning. Ownership does not become meaningful merely because replacement hardware can be purchased—the information and procedures needed to install that hardware must travel with it.

This is where secrecy and pairing reinforce each other. Withhold the pairing procedure, and an ordinary replacement becomes dependent on the manufacturer. Withhold the diagnostic documentation, and the shop may be unable to tell whether the problem lies in the part, the installation, or the authorization system (three very different repairs). The manual is the card catalog for the machine; without it, even a room full of parts isn't a useful library.

A practical disclosure rule should be narrow, predictable, and tied to real repair work. Manufacturers should provide independent shops and device owners with the same service information, diagnostic software, pairing procedures, and calibration tools offered to authorized repairers (on fair and reasonable terms). The rule should not require disclosure of source code, unrelated trade secrets, or information that has no repair function. It should cover whatever is necessary to restore a device to working order—including the ability to install and authenticate a lawful replacement part—because access that stops at the screwdriver leaves the central barrier intact.

The rule also needs a simple test. If a replacement sensor is installed, can you obtain the documented steps required to calibrate it? If access depends on discretionary approval, an unavailable account, or a tool priced to exclude ordinary shops, disclosure hasn't occurred. Security concerns may justify safeguards for genuinely sensitive functions, but they aren't a blank check for hiding routine service procedures. The safeguard must address the specific risk while leaving lawful repair possible.

We need lawmakers to treat repair information as part of the practical infrastructure of ownership. A disclosure duty should be enforceable, should cover updates that change repair procedures, and should prohibit technical locks that defeat the disclosed instructions. Agencies responsible for implementing such a rule should publish clear compliance guidance and provide a usable complaint process.

Disclosure will not erase every advantage held by a manufacturer or a large service chain. It will give independent shops a fair chance to compete on skill, price, and service rather than on privileged access to a locked cabinet. That is the public-interest principle: when a company sells a repairable product, our law should require it to release the tools of understanding needed to keep that product working. Ask representatives to put that rule into right-to-repair legislation.
```
