```markdown
Public records that only the affluent can afford to read are public in the same sense that a park is public when the gate charges a month’s rent. The courthouse says you may inspect the file, but the usable digital copy sits behind a private tollbooth. If your job, housing, lawsuit, or reputation turns on that file, theoretical access won’t help you. Your right has been converted into a price list – and we should call that what it is.

Digital court access matters because court records are where state power leaves receipts: complaints, motions, orders, judgments. A paper room open during business hours may satisfy an old rule, but it doesn’t satisfy the person working those same hours, living three counties away, or using a screen reader (accessibility isn’t a luxury add-on). When you must hire a database company merely to discover what your courts have done, public oversight has acquired a reseller. That reseller may offer a better product, but the public institution has left the democratic function unfinished: publication without practical discovery is a library whose books have been shelved by color, then handed to a toll collector for indexing and retrieval services.

Consider the machinery. Courts create the records with public authority. Clerks organize them. Taxpayers support the institution. Then a private database gathers the material, improves the search, and rents the result back at professional rates (the search layer is the valuable layer). You supplied the raw material; your ability to find it is sold separately – like buying a car and learning that the steering wheel requires a subscription.

There is a limited truth here: private services clean data, link dockets, and build powerful tools. Fair enough. Those services can charge for analysis, alerts, annotations, bulk workflows, and polished interfaces. But the underlying record shouldn’t become their captive input. A bakery may charge for cake; it can’t buy the town’s grain silo and then invoice every resident for flour (even if its frosting is excellent). The distinction isn’t hostile to business – it’s the condition that keeps business from owning the law’s memory.

The inequality compounds. If you can search broadly, compare cases, and follow a dispute over time, you can spot patterns. If you can’t, each docket looks isolated (a single loose gear rather than a machine). Employers, landlords, reporters, researchers, and large firms can buy visibility; an ordinary defendant may get a narrow window and a bewildering interface. Your legal rights don’t shrink, formally, but your practical power does. That gap is bullshit, and it teaches us that transparency distributed by purchasing power is another name for privilege.

I start from a simple test. For me, the answer turns on whether my judgment can be checked against the record without buying admission from a gatekeeper. I don’t mean that every document must be effortless or costless. Privacy redactions, security, maintenance, and careful indexing are real work. The point is who carries the baseline burden.

What should baseline access look like? Courts should publish timely, searchable records in open, documented formats; waive charges for ordinary lookup and modest downloading; provide accessible interfaces; and permit independent public-interest tools to copy and index the material. That lets you inspect the law without asking a reseller for permission. Sensitive information still needs lawful redaction, and exceptional costs may justify narrow fees. But a fee should reflect a service actually requested, rather than function as a moat around information already produced by public power.

Private databases could then compete above a public floor rather than substitute for one. They could sell speed, integration, prediction, and convenience. They wouldn’t get to sell the fact that a case exists. When access begins with a credit card, transparency is merely a premium product. We can choose a different rule: the public record should be practically public, and our courts should deliver it in a form available to all of us.
```
