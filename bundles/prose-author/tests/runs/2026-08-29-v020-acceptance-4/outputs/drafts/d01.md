```markdown
A device you buy isn't fully yours if its maker can reach through the sale and switch off a feature. Ownership is supposed to end the seller’s authority over ordinary use. When that authority survives (hidden in an account, a server check, or a license), the transaction resembles a car sale in which the dealer keeps a second key. You paid for the machine; somebody else kept the lock.

What's sold in such a bargain? On its face, everything looks normal: money crosses the counter, the box enters your home, and the receipt calls the device property. But if a remote command can subtract capacity, compatibility, repair access, or a paid service, the maker hasn't transferred control – it has rented stability back to you. That's the trick. The object sits in your hand while a revocable permission sits inside the object (and permission belongs to whoever can revoke it).

Sure, remote administration can have legitimate uses. A security patch can protect a machine; a network service may need abuse controls; a subscription can expire under terms disclosed before purchase. Those limited truths don't settle the ownership question. The crucial issue is whether the vendor can alter the durable capabilities of purchased hardware after taking the purchase price (especially when continued function depends on its servers). A useful service adds value. A private kill switch preserves leverage – over repair, resale, competition, and when working products become waste.

I find the car-key comparison useful because it keeps the layers straight. The machine is the car, software is the ignition, authentication is the key, and the maker’s server is the hand that may turn it. If I can sell the chassis but the next owner must beg for a fresh key, my title is thinner than the metal suggests. If I can't repair the ignition without permission, ownership has become a costume. The bullshit begins when that dependency is described as mere convenience rather than retained power.

This distinction matters because digital control scales cheaply. A locksmith must visit one car; a vendor can change thousands of devices with one policy. That asymmetry changes the bargain after checkout – the buyer bears sunk costs while the maker keeps a cheap threat. You may accept an update because refusing it risks security, compatibility, or access. You may accept new terms because the alternative is a crippled product you've already paid for. Consent delivered under that architecture isn't much of a shield (a button can record agreement without producing a fair choice).

The policy answer shouldn't ban updates or connected services. It should require an honest separation between property and ongoing service. Core functions promised at sale should keep working locally when the service ends. Owners should be able to authorize repair, install lawful replacement software, transfer the device, and refuse feature-reducing changes. If a function genuinely requires outside labor or infrastructure, that dependency should be stated before payment in language you can understand and priced as a service rather than smuggled into the object.

We need remedies that follow the power. When a company disables a purchased feature, buyers deserve restoration, refunds, and tools that let us route around the failed service. Interoperability and repair rights turn exit from a slogan into an option. We can't call a market competitive when every door out is controlled by the firm that built the room. And we shouldn't let a contract convert a product claim into a temporary favor.

The test is simple. After sale, ask whether you can use, fix, and transfer the device without the maker’s leave. If the answer depends on its grace, your ownership is a hostage with a receipt rather than a machine in the ordinary sense. We can allow useful services while insisting that what you bought remains under your control. Cut the leash: give owners the keys, repair access, and command of the thing in their hands.
```
