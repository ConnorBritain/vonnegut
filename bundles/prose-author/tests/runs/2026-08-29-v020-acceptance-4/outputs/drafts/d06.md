# The server was part of the appliance

When a vendor switches off the cloud service behind a connected appliance, it isn't merely ending a website. It is removing a working part from a machine already sold. You bought the box, paid for the chips, placed it beside your router, and supplied the electricity. The server was part of that appliance too. It was an undocumented component (one you could neither inspect nor replace), and the vendor has carried it out of your house through the network. The casing remains. That's remote repossession with better typography.

What stops working? Your doorbell may lose alerts, clips, or account login; your thermostat may lose schedules and automation; your lights may lose shared scenes. You can still press a button (a mercy marketed as resilience), but you can't use the app that made the product worth choosing. Soon you'll discover that your routines, integrations, voice commands, and access rules depended on a distant database, including features you paid for. The motor doesn't fail – the permission slip does. A sensor may keep sensing while the system won't report the result.

Did you buy an appliance, or did you unknowingly rent a favorable decision from a company that had already banked your money? The shutdown changes the original bargain because the purchase price covered capabilities whose continued existence the seller could later revoke. The vendor may have disclosed a cloud requirement in small print, but disclosure alone doesn't convert ownership into a revocable license. The decisive fact is control: after payment, the vendor still holds the switch and the customer still bears the loss.

I don't need a disaster story to recognize this arrangement. I can read the dependency graph (the useful question, not the branding). A product that phones home before performing its basic function splits ownership between buyer and vendor. I call that a tethered sale, a transaction dressed as a transfer. I can't make the server durable by believing the marketing, and I don't accept its disappearance as luck. The point isn't suspicion of networks; it's that a core function should survive the seller.

The cloud service is a rented workshop bolted to a purchased kitchen. The oven sits at home, but the ignition key hangs in the landlord's office; the timer consults a clerk; the recipe book is locked away. When the office closes, the kitchen becomes a steel sculpture. The parts explain the trick – the buyer owns the metal, while the vendor owns the approvals that make it useful. Calling this a graceful sunset is bullshit. It is a foreclosure against machinery whose purchase was supposedly complete.

Cloud services can add value: outside alerts, device coordination, demanding computation, and updates. A tiny appliance may reasonably borrow power from a large server. But that limited truth leaves shutdown risk unresolved. Optional cloud enhancement is one thing; mandatory cloud permission for basic operation is another. If the service is load-bearing, closing it is closer to removing a compressor from a refrigerator than discontinuing a newsletter. Convenience leaves the dependency intact; it merely perfumes it.

Local operation cuts the single corporate switch out of the basic function. Commands can travel across the home network; schedules can live on the device; accounts and keys can remain under household control. A local interface (documented and usable without vendor approval) lets independent software replace a discontinued app. Cloud features may disappear, but heating, lighting, locks, cameras, sensors, and switches retain their basic jobs. Hardware still fails and compatibility ends; local operation does something narrower – it separates an appliance's useful life from a vendor's appetite, acquisition, or bankruptcy.

We should treat the server as part of the appliance whenever a device depends on it for an advertised function. Our laws and purchasing standards can demand a local mode, documented interfaces, and a shutdown plan before sale. Then we can choose cloud convenience without accepting captivity. The remedy asks manufacturers to deliver what the box and price imply: an appliance that keeps faith after the account server goes dark. That obligation belongs to all of us because our homes are filling with property governed as a service. The server was part of the appliance. A sale should include the whole appliance.
