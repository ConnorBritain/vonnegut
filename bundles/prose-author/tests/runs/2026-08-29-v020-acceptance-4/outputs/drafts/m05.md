# Choice screens are not market choice

Choice screens are often presented as a modest competition measure: show people several services, let them pick, and trust the market to do the rest. But the decisive mechanism sits one step earlier. Someone chooses which services appear, what information accompanies them, and which option receives the most favorable position. The screen may display choice while the gatekeeper still designs the field (and keeps the gate).

That selection power reaches beyond the obvious act of excluding a rival. A platform can define eligibility (technical integration, billing support, safety review), decide when an option qualifies (before launch or after delay), and control the labels used to describe it. Even a neutral-looking list can reflect those upstream decisions. If the same company writes the entrance rules, applies them, and arranges the display, it hasn't surrendered control—it has converted control into interface design.

Defaults then turn that designed field into behavior. The first position, a highlighted button, or a preselected service (the default) reduces the effort required to continue. Alternatives ask for another tap, another decision, perhaps another warning. None of those burdens must be dramatic to matter; small frictions accumulate. What can you reasonably call a free selection when one path is prepared and every other path is work?

This doesn't mean every default is abusive. Defaults can reduce confusion and help a device function immediately (especially during setup). But convenience is also leverage, and the leverage belongs to whoever chooses the starting point. A remedy that requires a menu but leaves placement, wording, prompts, and fallback behavior untouched may preserve the old advantage beneath a new layer of compliance. That's a card catalog pasted over a database—the visible labels change, while the operator still controls retrieval.

Nominal choice can therefore stabilize gatekeeper power rather than dilute it. The listed providers compete for selection, but the gatekeeper controls access to the contest (including updates and removal). It can also collect information about which options succeed and revise the screen accordingly (a feedback advantage). The rule does not have to ban competitors outright if it leaves one company with the authority to shape visibility, friction, and timing. A marketplace managed this way isn't open merely because several names appear on it.

Meaningful relief would move power at each stage. An independent authority should set clear eligibility criteria (with public reasons for rejection), require genuinely equal presentation, and test the complete flow rather than the first screen alone. The default should be chosen by the device owner or set through a neutral process—not inherited from the gatekeeper's commercial arrangement. Switching must remain practical after setup, and deletion cannot break unrelated functions. Data generated by the selection process should remain unavailable as a private tool for steering future competition.

The remedy must also constrain sequence and repetition. A balanced opening screen means little if later updates repeatedly promote the gatekeeper's service, if warnings make switching look dangerous, or if a reset quietly restores the favored option. The relevant unit is the whole relationship between platform and buyer, from initial setup through updates, migration, and removal. Rules should cover ranking criteria, prompts, interoperability, data portability, and the ability of an independent service to reach the same system functions on fair terms. Otherwise the nominal remedy governs a single moment while leaving the durable sources of control intact. That limited intervention may change appearances while preserving the same incentives and dependencies.

Enforcement matters as much as screen design. Regulators need audit access, complaint procedures, prompt correction powers, and consequences strong enough to stop delay from becoming policy—compliance has to arrive while competition is still possible. We can't judge success by counting buttons; the test is whether the gatekeeper can determine who reaches buyers. Legislators may call a menu competition, but a menu isn't a market when the gatekeeper controls admission, ranking, defaults, and escape. Effective oversight should publish decisions, track steering, and allow providers to challenge treatment before exclusion becomes irreversible. Those safeguards protect more than rival companies; they protect the buyer's ability to choose an alternative that can function. We should require the institution that controls the platform to give up those powers—because competition begins only when the selector can't select the winner.
