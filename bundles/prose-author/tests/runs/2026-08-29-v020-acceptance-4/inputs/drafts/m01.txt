When a company sells a device while retaining the power to disable one of its features, the transaction carries a concealed cost. The buyer receives the hardware (and perhaps a perpetual-looking interface), but the maker keeps a switch that can change what the product does after money changes hands. That isn't full ownership. It is ownership with a remote-controlled exception—and the exception belongs to the seller.

Software complicates an old rule, but it doesn't erase it. Ownership has always included limits (safety rules, nuisance laws, and other people's rights), yet those limits come from law and coexistence, not from a former owner's continuing command. A maker's kill switch is different. It lets one party complete the sale and preserve a piece of control—without preserving the buyer's ability to bargain over each later change.

The distinction matters even when the disabled function seems minor (a color mode, a sensor, or an automated setting). Price reflects the bundle of functions offered at sale. If one side can later subtract from that bundle, the original bargain isn't stable; it is a revocable license wearing the shell of a purchase. A deed with rooms that the previous owner may lock at will wouldn't be called complete title.

Some remote controls serve legitimate purposes. A stolen device may need an account lock (triggered by the current owner), and dangerous network behavior may justify a narrowly defined intervention. But a legitimate goal does not settle who holds the authority, what process governs it, or whether the feature can be restored. The policy question goes beyond whether software can alter hardware. It plainly can. The question is who gets the final say.

That allocation should be settled before sale, in terms ordinary buyers can understand (including the duration and scope of any remote power). Fine print can't transform a product into a service whenever control becomes inconvenient to surrender. Nor should consent be manufactured by an update screen—accept the new terms or lose a function already purchased. A choice presented after payment, with the product's usefulness at stake, isn't meaningful bargaining.

A useful legal rule would separate temporary access from permanent authority (repair access differs from ownership), require clear disclosure before purchase, and make core functionality durable. If a feature is advertised as part of the device, the seller shouldn't be able to remove it merely because a server changes, a business model shifts, or support becomes expensive. The obligation cannot be an indefinite promise to improve everything. It can be a firm bar against unilateral subtraction.

Contract law can support that result, but product rules must make the baseline unmistakable. The burden belongs with the party preserving remote power, because that party designs the system, writes the terms, and controls the servers. Buyers lack realistic means to inspect each dependency at checkout. Clear defaults turn ownership from a stack of permissions into a durable allocation of authority.

The same rule needs remedies with teeth (refunds, restoration, and repair access where feasible). Otherwise disclosure becomes a smokescreen: a dense notice records the maker's power while leaving the buyer with no practical alternative. Regulators may be tempted to treat advance notice as sufficient, but notice alone won't rebalance a completed sale. What good is a warning if every comparable device reserves the same switch?

Lawmakers should define ownership around durable control (not merely physical possession), prohibit undisclosed remote disablement, and require a meaningful remedy when a promised feature disappears. We should also protect independent repair and interoperability, because a device that depends entirely on the maker's permission remains tethered long after checkout. These rules needn't ban security updates or owner-requested locks—they should put those powers in service of the purchaser. If your representative is considering digital ownership legislation, ask for that principle in the text. Our public interest is simple: a sale must transfer meaningful control, and a seller will not retain a hidden veto.
