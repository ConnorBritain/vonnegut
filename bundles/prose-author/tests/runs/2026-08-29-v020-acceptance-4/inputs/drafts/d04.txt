An online age check can become identity infrastructure for the same reason a nightclub wristband can become a passport: the useful part isn't the moment at the door, but the reusable proof that follows. A law may call the system a narrow safety measure. The machinery still creates an answer that websites, platforms, payment firms, and governments can ask for again.

Suppose you visit a site with restricted material. The site asks you to prove an age threshold, perhaps through a credential provider (a company that may know far more than the site does). You receive a token, your browser presents it, and the door opens. On its face, that's tidy: the site doesn't need your birthday, and the provider doesn't need to reveal it. But the token has done something larger – it has made you legible to a gate.

What happens when another service wants the same assurance? The cheapest answer won't be a fresh, isolated check; it'll be the credential already in circulation. Soon the wristband isn't proving only age. It can become an account-recovery key, an app-store permission slip, or a condition for entering a discussion (all under the soothing label “safety”). Each new use makes refusal costlier, because your access now depends on “you are over the line” remaining attached to your activities.

I don't need to assume a secret master plan to see the danger. I only need to follow the incentives. A verifier gains value when more doors accept its credential; a gatekeeper saves money when it reuses a familiar check; an authority gains leverage when access decisions can be audited from one layer. My concern begins with that alignment, rather than a villain's memo. It tells me where the machine rolls when everyone pushes in the easiest direction.

Of course, age limits can serve legitimate purposes, and a system can minimize some disclosures. Data minimization matters (a yes-or-no answer can expose less than a birth date). But minimization at one transaction doesn't settle the architecture around it. We should ask who issues the proof, who can demand it, who records each presentation, and whether the credential can be correlated across contexts. Privacy isn't merely the size of one data packet – it's the power created by repeated demands.

Identity infrastructure has three layers. First comes enrollment: some actor accepts evidence and creates a durable credential. Next comes presentation: many services learn to request the credential in a standard form. Finally comes governance: rules decide whose proof counts, which failures trigger exclusion, and which records survive. Each layer can be narrow in isolation while the stack becomes general. The system resembles a rail network assembled one spur at a time, with control accumulating at the junctions.

When a credential becomes customary, consent gets theatrical. You may technically decline, but then you can't enter the store, forum, classroom, or service. Your choice resembles declining a bank card in a town that has stopped taking cash (a freedom with nowhere to stand). Worse, a breach or mistaken denial no longer blocks one doorway; it can shadow you from door to door. The system turns a fact about you into a switch over a person's life.

That is why we should treat age-checking proposals as infrastructure bills, even when their titles promise something smaller. We need strict limits on reuse, linking, retention, and compelled presentation; independent routes for people who lack documents; and a real way to challenge errors. We should require technical separation between checks, because policy promises can evaporate while identifiers persist. Above all, we should reject any design whose easiest future is universal use. Otherwise the narrow gate becomes the central checkpoint.

The wristband belongs at one door, for one purpose, and for one visit. Once it opens every door, it is no longer a safety accessory. It is identity infrastructure – and calling it narrow is just the damn sales pitch printed on the band.
