### Observation instructions

- o01 [person-reader-stance; section:address; measurement:second-person-family]: Direct address appears chiefly when the argument turns into practical civic action or asks readers to imagine how a rule operates. Use “you” near a concrete consequence or closing invitation, keeping the main policy analysis in third person rather than sustaining a conversational address across the supported contexts.
- o02 [person-reader-stance, self-reference-biography; section:address; measurement:first-person-plural-family]: The plural voice usually represents EFF as an institutional analyst, advocate, or participant, though an occasional inclusive “us all” broadens the threatened public. Use “we” to identify research, contact, belief, or action by the organization; make broader inclusion explicit rather than leaving its scope ambiguous.
- o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contractions keep statutory and technical explanation in an accessible blog register, especially in blunt rebuttals such as “that’s” or “doesn’t.” Use them in transitions, consequence statements, and plain-language glosses of legal claims, allowing the prose to sound spoken without turning casual or chatty.
- o04 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negative forms give categorical legal, factual, or normative claims extra weight: a bill “does not” solve a problem, government “should not” impose a system, or evidence “cannot” be obtained. Reserve the expanded form for firm limits and conclusions rather than mechanically replacing ordinary contractions.
- o05 [self-reference-biography; section:absences; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: Individual self-reference recedes behind the organization’s analysis; personal experience and biography do not carry the case. Frame knowledge, advocacy, and judgment through EFF, an institutional “we,” named evidence, or the policy’s observable effects, using singular self-reference only when a genuinely personal standpoint is indispensable.
- o06 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions expose a bill’s unresolved mechanics or convert an abstract speech risk into concrete examples readers can test. Place a short sequence after explaining the disputed rule, or use one framing question in an opening; answer through the surrounding analysis rather than inviting an open-ended exchange.
- o07 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses insert bill abbreviations, statutory definitions, examples, and narrow factual qualifications without diverting the main claim. Use them for compact decoding or scope control inside explanatory sentences; keep the central consequence outside the brackets so the argument remains legible to a general reader.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:em-dashes]: Em dashes drive pivots from official description to practical consequence, isolate a decisive example, or sharpen the last clause of a sentence. Use them where the prose needs an audible turn—especially from claimed protection to actual burden—while keeping the interruption tightly connected to the governing claim.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:en-dashes]: Range-like or interruptive work is carried more clearly by words, parentheses, or em dashes than by en dashes. Prefer those established devices for age ranges, asides, and pivots; treat an en dash as a specialized typographic choice rather than a general-purpose mark in drafting.
- o10 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Concessions acknowledge a legitimate goal or a partial legislative improvement before narrowing the dispute to implementation, incentives, or remaining harms. Predictions are tied to practical pressure with “may,” “likely,” or “could.” Place qualification just before the rebuttal so it demonstrates scrutiny without weakening the eventual conclusion.
- o11 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Legislators, bill supporters, agencies, companies, and platforms are named by role, then assessed through text, incentives, history, or reported conduct rather than attributed motives. Allies and outside authorities receive explicit credit. Introduce a source at the point it substantiates a factual premise, then state the policy inference separately.
- o12 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures come from familiar systems and physical consequences: a card catalog instead of a database, a valuable data vault, a dragnet, a death sentence, or a movie seen before. Use one compact analogy after the mechanism is established, chiefly to make administrative burden or predictable incentives tangible.
- o13 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Openings name the bill or contested practice, summarize its stated aim, and pivot quickly to the concealed mechanism or harm. Section endings condense a causal consequence in plain language. Close by naming the responsible institution, issuing a specific demand, and often returning to the opening values of privacy, speech, access, or competition.
