- o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contractions keep explanations conversational even when the subject is statutory language or institutional procedure. - o04 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negative forms give categorical findings and recommendations extra weight: a bill does not solve the stated problem, protections are not sufficient, or lawmakers should not proceed. - o05 [self-reference-biography; section:absences; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: Individual self-reference yields to the institutional plural, keeping attention on public consequences rather than personal experience. - o06 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions dramatize practical contradictions and test a proposal against ordinary conduct rather than soliciting answers. Cluster them after explaining a vague legal category, then let the following prose supply the consequence. They can also headline an investigation, but should not replace the article’s declarative policy case.
- o07 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses efficiently supply bill abbreviations, statutory definitions, examples, and compact clarifications without diverting the main claim. Use them inside explanatory passages when a reader needs a precise label or boundary; keep the enclosed material factual and brief enough that the sentence’s argumentative line remains visible.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:em-dashes]: Em dashes sharpen pivots, insert consequential examples, and recast an abstract rule in plainer terms. They often make the second phrase more pointed—identifying who bears a burden or what a disclaimer conceals. Use them for a compact argumentative turn, not for loosely related digressions.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:en-dashes]: Ranges and interruptions are ordinarily handled through words, parentheses, or em dashes rather than en dashes. Prefer the em dash when a clause needs a forceful qualification or reveal, and use ordinary range syntax for dates and ages unless source terminology requires a typographically fixed form.
- o10 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Concessions acknowledge a legitimate aim, a real harm, or an improved amendment before separating that merit from the proposal’s defects. Predictions are tied to incentives with modals such as “may,” “could,” and “likely.” Place the concession before the rebuttal, then explain the causal path rather than merely sounding cautious.
- o11 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Bills, agencies, companies, and supporters are named directly, while motives receive less attention than legal text and predictable incentives. Sources enter as linked reports, court decisions, statutory language, coalition letters, or an attributed response. Credit allies specifically, quote opponents fairly, and then identify the unresolved flaw or consequence.
- o12 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures come from familiar systems and physical consequences: a card catalog instead of a database, a vault of personal information, a dragnet, or a movie seen before. Extend the comparison only far enough to clarify administrative burden, accumulated risk, or repeated failure, and land it beside concrete evidence.
- o13 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Openings name the live bill, vote, or reported practice and quickly state the hidden consequence behind its stated purpose. Sections often end with a short causal verdict. Final paragraphs compress the preferred alternative and issue a direct institutional demand, sometimes returning to an earlier public-interest phrase for closure.
