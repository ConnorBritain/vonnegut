- o06 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions dramatize practical contradictions and test a proposal against ordinary conduct rather than soliciting answers. - o07 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses efficiently supply bill abbreviations, statutory definitions, examples, and compact clarifications without diverting the main claim. - o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:em-dashes]: Em dashes sharpen pivots, insert consequential examples, and recast an abstract rule in plainer terms. - o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:en-dashes]: Ranges and interruptions are ordinarily handled through words, parentheses, or em dashes rather than en dashes. - o11 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Bills, agencies, companies, and supporters are named directly, while motives receive less attention than legal text and predictable incentives. - o12 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures come from familiar systems and physical consequences: a card catalog instead of a database, a vault of personal information, a dragnet, or a movie seen before. - o13 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Openings name the live bill, vote, or reported practice and quickly state the hidden consequence behind its stated purpose. Sections often end with a short causal verdict. Final paragraphs compress the preferred alternative and issue a direct institutional demand, sometimes returning to an earlier public-interest phrase for closure.
