```markdown
Ownership is a simple idea until a corporation decides it isn't.

When you buy a device – a tractor, a phone, an insulin pump – the transaction feels complete. Money changes hands, the thing is yours, and what happens next is your business. That's the story we've always told about property. It's also not true anymore, and I think we need to stop pretending it is.

Think of it like buying a house where the builder retains the right to seal off your basement whenever they feel like it. You paid full price, you have the deed, but there's a clause (buried in the terms-of-service you clicked through at 11pm) that says the seller can reach back in and remove something whenever it's profitable. That's not ownership. That's a rental with a one-time upfront fee and a landlord who never has to call ahead.

John Deere understood this before most. When a farmer buys a $500,000 combine, they're not buying the software that runs it – Deere retains that as a "license." So when the machine breaks mid-harvest (and it will, because everything breaks), the farmer can't fix it themselves. They have to wait for an authorized technician while the crop rots. Deere's position is that you bought the iron and they kept the machine. The right-to-repair movement has been fighting this for years, and I think it's still losing.

Apple does the same thing, more politely. Software updates that throttle older phones (Apple called this "performance management," which is a genuinely impressive piece of language), features switched on and off via server-side flags, pairing requirements that cause a genuine replacement part – pulled from a genuine Apple device – to get rejected by another genuine Apple device. What does ownership mean if the seller can quietly remove capabilities to make next year's model look better by comparison? The company's answer, when pressed, is that all of this protects you. But we know it's about controlling the aftermarket and extracting rent from every repair shop that didn't pay for the privilege, because the moment right-to-repair laws started passing, companies suddenly discovered that independent repair was safe after all.

Here's what's actually going on: a device whose features can be altered after sale by the seller is not a product. It's a service agreement dressed in a product's clothes. Service agreements come with ongoing obligations, ongoing relationships, ongoing leverage. The company can degrade your device if you don't upgrade. They can brick a feature because you used a part they didn't authorize. They can quietly strip out capabilities to drive you toward the new model. Every one of those moves is something a landlord does, not a seller – and we've been letting companies act like landlords while charging us like sellers and getting away with it completely.

The remedies aren't complicated, even if getting there is. We should treat post-sale feature removal as a product defect, the same way we'd treat a car manufacturer secretly disabling your airbags. Software licenses should convey actual use rights to the buyer, not a revocable permission the seller can yank back whenever it's convenient. And we should tax the shit out of companies that sell you a device and then charge you again to keep it working – because what they've sold isn't a device, it's a dependency.

What you bought should be what you own. The moment a company can reach back through the network and take something away, the sale was a fiction.
```
