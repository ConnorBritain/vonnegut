Accuracy and invasiveness are the same dial. Not two dials that happen to sit side by side on the same console, not a tradeoff that some sufficiently clever engineer is going to escape: one dial, with a friendly word painted on one side and an ugly one painted on the other, and the whole privacy-preserving adtech industry is the business of making sure you only ever read the friendly side. An ad is accurate exactly to the degree that it knows something about you that it couldn't have guessed from the page you're reading, and everything it knows that it couldn't guess from the page had to come from somewhere else: a checkout, a pharmacy, a Saturday afternoon you spent reading about a diagnosis you haven't told your family about yet. Precision is a polite word for how much of your life is in the file.

Think about resolution. A photograph of a crowd at low resolution tells you there are people in the square; at high resolution it tells you that the woman in the green coat has been crying. Nothing was added to the square. The only thing that changed is how much of her the picture holds, and there is no lens setting that hands you her face and her privacy at once. Targeting is that picture. Google, Meta, the IAB and every exchange in between would like to sell you the version where the crowd gets sharper while the woman gets blurrier, and no such lens exists, because the sharpness of the crowd is made out of the sharpness of her face.

So what are Topics, the Privacy Sandbox and on-device inference actually doing? They're moving where the computation happens, not how much of you it needs. The Sandbox pitch is that your own browser does the sorting, so no server ever sees your history: true, and irrelevant to the dial, because a browser that can drop you in a useful bucket is a browser that has read everything you read in order to do it. It knows you the way your dog knows the sound of your car. Apple's App Tracking Transparency didn't shrink the file either; it changed who's allowed to hold it, which is a real improvement and a different improvement. A clean room is a room. The data still walks in.

Ask a vendor one question: if your targeting got less invasive, what got worse? There is always an answer and it is always the same — match rates, lift, attribution — which is to say that the thing the advertiser is paying for got worse in exact proportion to the amount of you that got withheld. That's not a bug in the pitch. That's the pitch confessing. So when a company announces it has improved both at once, it has either redefined one of the words or it has moved the invasion somewhere the measurement doesn't look: onto the handset, into a hashed identifier, into a partner's clean room, into a model's weights where nobody can point at a row and say that row is you.

Where does the file go when the industry tells you the file is gone? Into the weights, mostly, and that is where this stops being optimism and starts being a con. An identifier can be deleted and a training set can in principle be forgotten; a model that has learned you has no row to delete, so the file is declared destroyed while the inference sits there, warm, ready to reconstitute you out of three clicks. They know this. It's the whole fucking point: launder the file into a form the law hasn't learned to name yet.

Which is why we should stop arguing about mechanisms and start arguing about the dial. We have spent years being invited into a debate about cookies versus fingerprints, server-side versus on-device — a debate about lens coatings — and it's one we lose by entering, because entering concedes that the amount of us inside the system is an implementation detail rather than the entire question. We don't have to concede that. The question is how much of you the system may hold at all, and that question has an answer in law and no answer in engineering.

Privacy-preserving is a decent phrase, and I think most of the engineers using it mean it. It just doesn't mean what you'd assume: it preserves your privacy the way a jar preserves a peach. The peach is still in there, sealed and indexed and shelf-stable, waiting on whoever owns the pantry — and the better the preservation, the longer they keep you.
