## 1. Cadence

**Contraction and negation.** Contractions support an accessible advocacy register, especially in explanatory transitions and direct claims: “it’s,” “won’t,” “doesn’t,” and “shouldn’t” keep sentences brisk without making them casual. Use them through ordinary exposition and calls to action; reserve more formal phrasing for quoted legal language or carefully framed institutional claims.

## 2. How a piece opens

**Openings, paragraph endings, and closure.** Openings state the bill, event, or disputed practice immediately, then add the central harm in a compact contrast. Sections use descriptive problem headings, proceed from mechanism to consequence, and often close by naming the proper remedy: reject the bill, preserve oversight, narrow enforcement, or restore a less invasive practice. Place the call to action last.

Use “you” sparingly for a concrete next step, not as a continuous conversational frame; let most explanation proceed through institutions, lawmakers, platforms, and affected users.

## 5. Figures

**Figures and analogy vocabulary.** Figures make technical policy consequences concrete through familiar comparisons and compressed images: AI training is “like reading,” a data archive becomes a “valuable vault,” and a legal disclaimer is a “smokescreen.” Use one intelligible analogy after the mechanism is explained, then return to the rights, privacy, or competition consequence.

## 6. Register range

**Qualification and hedging.** The prose concedes limited improvements before preserving the central objection: a narrowed bill “does address some of the broadest concerns,” yet serious problems remain. It also marks uncertainty with “may,” “likely,” “could,” and “it’s unclear,” especially for legal consequences. Place concessions before the decisive public-interest conclusion.

Sources are credited with links and specific findings, while allies appear as organizations joining letters or advocacy. Introduce outside claims, then explain their consequence.
