### Observation instructions

- o01 [person-reader-stance; section:address; measurement:second-person-family]: Direct second-person address places the reader inside the argument as a participant, witness, or person receiving practical instruction. It turns abstract systems into immediate stakes and often sharpens an accusation or invitation. Use it for consequential examples, reader-facing explanations, and calls to inspect familiar arrangements.
- o02 [person-reader-stance; section:address; measurement:first-person-plural-family]: First-person plural language builds a shared public viewpoint, usually grouping writer and reader against institutions, technical systems, or harmful defaults. It makes analysis collaborative without softening blame. Use we and our when naming collective exposure, shared obligations, or a common opportunity to resist.
- o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contractions keep exposition conversational and quick, including in serious political, economic, and technical claims. They help the prose move from explanation into direct address or indignation without a formal reset. Prefer the contracted form in ordinary narration and argument; reserve fuller phrasing for deliberate emphasis.
- o04 [contraction-negation; section:absences; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negative forms appear as occasional points of emphasis within otherwise conversational prose. They slow a sentence and give a denial, correction, or boundary extra weight. Use an uncontracted negative when rejecting a premise or marking a firm distinction; keep routine negation in the surrounding conversational register.
- o05 [profanity-vulgarity; section:register-range; measurement:profanity-vulgarity]: Profanity and vulgarity function as pointed intensifiers, comic deflation, or moral verdicts rather than as a continuous baseline. They cluster around hypocrisy, coercion, exploitation, and absurd technical or bureaucratic behavior. Use a blunt term at a pressure point, where it clarifies the scale or ridiculousness of the offense.
- o06 [self-reference-biography; section:register-range; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: The singular first person appears as an involved arguer and occasional witness, supplying remembered encounters, personal reactions, or a bridge into a broader claim. It does not turn the piece into memoir. Use I to establish how an example entered the argument, then return promptly to the public stakes.
- o07 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions are used rhetorically to expose contradictions, test a sales pitch, or pivot from an apparent explanation toward the underlying power relation. They often anticipate the reader’s objection before answering it. Place a question after the setup of a false premise, then follow with a concrete demonstration.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses carry compact asides, qualifications, comic corrections, and supplementary naming without fully interrupting the main line. They let the prose preserve a forward argument while briefly changing register. Use them for information that sharpens tone or context but would distract if promoted to a separate sentence.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:em-dashes]: The corpus does not establish em dashes as a supported interruption mark for this register. Use the documented alternatives for pivots, asides, and qualifications: parentheses for contained commentary and en dashes where the prose needs a visible connective or contrast.
- o10 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:en-dashes]: En dashes support visible pivots, contrasts, and inserted relations within an otherwise continuous sentence. They are especially useful when a qualification or opposition should remain attached to the claim rather than become a parenthetical aside. Use them selectively for argumentative turns and compressed apposition.
- o11 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Qualification usually takes the form of explicit concessions, conditional frames, and calibrated limits before the argument returns to a firm conclusion. The prose distinguishes uncertainty from evasion by naming what remains true despite the caveat. Place a concession near the relevant claim, then state the practical or moral consequence plainly.
- o12 [opponents-allies-sources; section:register-range]: Opponents are commonly introduced through their slogans, categories, or quoted claims, then tested against examples and consequences. Allies and sources receive names, links, and a defined contribution; the writer often extends or corrects them rather than treating citation as decoration. Introduce a source at the point where its evidence advances the argument.
- o13 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures draw from ordinary bodily experience, games, commerce, folklore, computing, and familiar institutions. Comparisons are often extended across several sentences so an abstract power relation becomes visible, comic, and difficult to evade. Introduce the analogy after the problem is defined, develop its mechanism, and return explicitly to the case at hand.
- o14 [openings-endings-closure; section:openings]: Openings often begin with a quoted sentence, a definition, an irony, or a compact claim that creates immediate argumentative pressure. The body then widens through examples and linked evidence. Closures commonly compress the thesis into a memorable contrast or imperative, returning to the opening problem while adding a clear public consequence.
