# The server was part of the appliance.

An appliance that depends on a server has two bodies: the object in the house and the computer elsewhere that gives it permission to work. When you bought your connected lock, camera, thermostat, or speaker, the box wasn't the whole product (the software across the network was part of it). If the vendor closes that service, what exactly did you buy? You bought hardware whose useful life was tied to a promise hidden behind your wall and outside your control – a promise we call “the cloud” because “somebody else’s computer” sounds less magical.

Your thermostat may still display a temperature while schedules, remote controls, energy reports, geofencing, voice commands, household sharing, and security alerts disappear. A camera may record nothing because authentication lives on a dead server; a lock may open by keypad but can't issue guest codes; a light may illuminate but no longer run the routines you arranged. The plastic doesn't vanish. The capabilities do (and sometimes setup or account recovery goes with them), leaving your appliance as a smaller, stranger object.

That changes the original bargain. Payment ordinarily settles a transaction: money moves one way, control moves the other. That wasn't the deal here, although the sale looked like one. The vendor retained a private veto over the product after collecting the purchase price. It didn't need to enter the house or recall the hardware. It merely had to stop answering network requests, and the appliance wouldn't be the appliance anymore. When we lose functions because a distant computer goes dark, the shutdown reveals what was sold: conditional access dressed up as ownership.

I don't think every connected feature must run forever. Servers cost money, security work continues, and companies can fail. But those facts describe a maintenance problem; they don't settle who should bear the loss. I use a simple test: after service ends, does the essential function remain available without permission from the seller? If the answer is no, my purchase was really a tenancy with one enormous prepaid rent cheque (and a remote control held by the landlord) – except the landlord can demolish the rooms while the furniture remains.

Even if a shutdown is announced months ahead, notice alone doesn't repair the bargain. A long countdown merely tells you when your property will shrink. Exporting data may help, and refunds may soften the cost, but neither gives back the capabilities that weren't designed to survive independently. If replacement requires buying another device, the shutdown converts planned obsolescence into compulsory shopping. Calling that a “service transition” would be bullshit (the part undergoing transition is the buyer’s money) – the mechanism is remote repossession by software.

Local operation protects against that veto. Core controls, schedules, automations, recordings, and account administration should run inside our homes, with cloud connections adding convenience rather than granting permission. Remote access can relay encrypted messages to the appliance; it shouldn't be the appliance’s only brain. Published protocols and replaceable server settings would let another program take over when the original service ends. Exportable keys and documented recovery procedures matter too. Redundancy isn't glamorous, but a fire exit rarely is, and the point becomes obvious when the main door doesn't open.

Local operation also changes incentives. A shop that sells a drill cannot make the trigger stop working from headquarters, so its revenue plan must coexist with the drill’s continued existence. Connected products should face the same discipline. I can accept that optional hosting may expire; what matters to me is whether the machinery keeps performing its purchased job. The server may provide an extra entrance, a concierge, or a delivery window. It must never become the foundation that disappears while the building is occupied.

We should demand a plain rule from manufacturers and lawmakers: if closing a cloud service removes a material feature, the product must include a local path for that feature or a transferable path to another operator. Disclosure before sale helps, but disclosure alone turns dispossession into fine print. The durable protection is technical independence backed by an enforceable obligation. The server was part of the appliance, and our rule should treat its removal like removing any other essential component after the sale.
