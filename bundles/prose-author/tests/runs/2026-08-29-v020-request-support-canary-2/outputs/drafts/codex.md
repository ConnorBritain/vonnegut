The device is yours, until it isn't: a device is not fully owned when its maker can disable features after sale. That sounds like ownership with an escrow agent hiding in the paperwork, holding the useful parts of your purchase until somebody else decides you may have them. What exactly did you buy? A thing, or a permission slip?

You already know the old answer: ownership means control, within the ordinary limits of owning a physical object. You can lend it, repair it, resell it, or leave it in a drawer for ten years without asking the manufacturer's permission. A connected device changes that relationship – not because it has software, but because the software can become a remote hand on the switch. The box sits in your home; the decisive power sits somewhere else (a neat trick, if your goal is to sell the appearance of possession).

Imagine buying a refrigerator with an escrow agent who promises to release the freezer only while you remain in good standing with the appliance company. You'd call that a rental with a very expensive security deposit. You wouldn't accept a car dealer retaining the right to disable the brakes because your account had been closed, or a furniture store repossessing one chair while leaving the rest of the set in your dining room. Yet when the controlled object is digital, the same arrangement arrives dressed as an update, a service condition, or a small line in a contract nobody expects you to read.

Of course, the answer comes back that these features are merely licensed. Naturally, the device remains yours, they say, even if the function that made it worth buying can be withdrawn. But a license is being used here as a magic word – a way to turn a material loss of control into a technicality. If the maker can decide whether your device works, then your ownership is conditional, and the condition is enforced by the seller. That isn't a philosophical quibble; it's the difference between possessing a tool and renting access to one.

I think the cleanest way to see the problem is to follow the money. When you buy a device, you pay at the beginning for value you expect to receive over time. When the maker keeps the power to disable features, the purchase price starts looking like a tipped minimum wage for your own equipment – enough to get you through the transaction, never enough to give you independence. We are told that this arrangement protects security, improves quality, and keeps the product current. Those goals can matter, but they don't answer the ownership question.

Security isn't a blank check, and improvement isn't a synonym for obedience. A maker can offer updates without reserving an eternal veto over the buyer's use. We can demand durable local functionality, meaningful repair rights, clear expiration terms, and a way to keep using the features we paid for when the company changes its mind. If that sounds unreasonable, ask why the unreasonable party is the person who wants the thing they purchased to keep working.

The industry's favorite defense is that nobody is forcing you to buy the device. Fine: nobody is forcing the seller to call a revocable permission an owned product, either. We should be suspicious of any bargain that gives one side the money and the other side the kill switch. That isn't innovation; it's bullshit with a firmware update.

You don't need to reject every connected product to reject this model of ownership. You need to name the boundary clearly: a maker may build a service around a device, but it shouldn't be able to erase the device's promised value after the sale. We own the object only when our control survives the seller's next decision. Otherwise, the device is still in escrow.
