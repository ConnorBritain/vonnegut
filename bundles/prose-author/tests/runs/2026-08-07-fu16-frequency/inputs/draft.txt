The theory of the password manager goes like this: you are bad at secrets and computers are good at them, so you take the eleven passwords you actually use, hand them to a company whose entire business is holding secrets, and pay it a few dollars a month to hold yours so carefully that even it can't read them. That's the pitch, and the (very load-bearing) word in it is *can't*. Not *won't*. The vault gets sealed on your device, the key never crosses the wire, and what sits on the company's disks is a brick of noise the company itself couldn't open if a court asked nicely. It's a good deal. You just made it with a company that doesn't exist anymore.

Because the thing you bought was never the crypto. The crypto is a weekend of well-understood math that any competent team can implement, and most of them have. What you bought was everything wrapped around it: engineers paid enough to stay, a third-party audit that happens whether or not it's convenient, a breach disclosure that goes out in days instead of quarters, a free tier good enough for your mother, a client that gets patched when a researcher emails on a Friday night. None of that is in the source code. All of it is in the org chart. The org chart was the fucking product.

Think about the locksmith on your corner. He cuts you a key, and he swears — and he means it — that he keeps no copy. And he doesn't. His fingers, his machine, his blank; the copy exists in your pocket and nowhere else. You believe him because you've watched him work and because his kid goes to school with yours.

Now a man from three towns over buys the shop. Same sign, same counter, same little bell over the door. The locksmith stays on for eighteen months at a trimmed wage, and then he stays on as a consultant, and then he doesn't stay on. The machine is still there. The blanks are still there. But the new owner has a debt to service, and the shop itself is the collateral for the loan that bought the shop, so every quiet unbillable thing — sweeping the shavings, replacing the worn cutter, turning away the guy who wants a copy of somebody else's key — is now a line item with a person above it asking why it costs what it costs.

Nobody hands out your key. That was never the risk. The risk is that the shop stops being the kind of shop where handing it out is unthinkable.

Buy a password manager and the price goes up. Buy a password manager and the audit that used to be annual becomes an audit that happened once. Buy a password manager and the app quietly picks up a telemetry SDK, then an upsell modal, then a panel of recommended offers with a partner's tracking pixel in it — in the app that holds every credential you own.

So if the encryption really is end-to-end, why should any of this bother you? Because end-to-end encryption is a promise about the wire, not about the app on your side of it, and the app is compiled and signed and shipped to you by whoever owns the shop this quarter. The math is fine. The math was always going to be fine. The math has no opinion about what else gets bolted into the binary that runs it, and the rollup boys have never once looked at a working piece of software and failed to find a place to attach a fee.

I think the part that actually gets people isn't the decay, though. It's that the switching cost is the product. Getting out means exporting a plaintext file containing your entire life, walking it across a desktop, importing it somewhere else, and rotating everything you touched on the way — a weekend of tedious, high-stakes, unforgiving work, which is precisely why you won't do it, which is precisely why they don't have to worry about you doing it. A vault you can't leave isn't a vault. It's a hostage situation with a nice icon and a monthly invoice.

None of this requires anybody to break a promise. The promise was always narrow, and it was always about ciphertext, and it was never about ownership, or debt, or who gets to decide next year what ships inside the client. You checked the crypto. The crypto is not the part that changed hands.

And the sales copy still says zero knowledge, and it's still perfectly true, and it has quietly changed sides: the only zero knowledge left in this arrangement is yours.
