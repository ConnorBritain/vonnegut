Every password manager I've recommended in the last fifteen years has now been bought by someone whose business is not password management. Not one of these acquisitions was announced as a change to the product, and not one of them was a change to the product on day one, which is exactly how this works. The pitch is continuity: same app, same vault, same auditors, same founders staying on to "steward the mission." The pitch is always continuity. Then the founders' earnout vests and the roadmap starts to smell.

Here's the thing about a password manager that makes private equity's arrival so much worse than it would be for, say, a to-do list. You gave it everything. Not "some sensitive data" — everything: the bank, the mortgage servicer, the pharmacy, the ex's shared Netflix, the recovery codes for the email account that can reset all the rest of it. You did this because you were told to. Every security professional on Earth spent a decade screaming at you to stop reusing hunter2 and start using a manager, and you finally, dutifully, put your entire life inside one box with one door, and now the box has a new owner and nobody asked you about it:

https://example.com/pw-manager-acquisition

The security promise you agreed to was never really a promise about cryptography. The crypto's fine. The crypto is the least interesting part of this. What you actually agreed to was a promise about *incentives* — that the company holding the box would go on wanting the same thing you wanted, which is for the box to stay shut. Zero-knowledge architecture isn't a law of physics, it's a business decision expressed in code, and business decisions get revisited quarterly by people who did not make them.

Think about what a leveraged buyout actually is, because most people hear "acquisition" and picture a rich company buying a smaller one with its own money. That is not what happens. The PE firm borrows the purchase price, buys the company, and then — this is the part that should make you sit up — puts the *debt on the company it just bought*. Your password manager now owes several hundred million dollars it never borrowed, to service a loan it did not take out, for the privilege of having been purchased. Imagine buying a house by taking out a mortgage in the house's name, then charging the house rent, then firing the house's plumber to make the numbers work. That's the deal. The plumber, in this metaphor, is the four-person team that maintained the client-side encryption and did the annual third-party audit, and the numbers always, always work better without them.

So the pressure arrives from the balance sheet, not from a villain. Nobody in that building wakes up wanting to weaken your vault. They wake up needing eleven points of margin by Q3. Cancel the audit and you get some. Move the crypto team offshore and you get some more. Ship a "convenience" web vault that decrypts server-side because the mobile team can't keep two implementations in sync, and you get a whole lot — and you can even announce it as a feature. Every one of those decisions has a defensible memo behind it. None of them will be described to you as a downgrade.

And you will not be able to leave, which is the actual product being sold here. A password manager is the purest lock-in machine ever devised, because the switching cost isn't the export file — the export works fine, they'll cheerfully hand you a CSV of your entire life in plaintext — it's the eight hundred saved logins, the browser extension your parents finally learned, the shared family vault, the 2FA seeds that don't export at all. These vultures know that. It's priced in. The stickiness *is* the asset, and stickiness is just a friendly word for the fact that you can't get out.

Which is why the answer isn't a better vendor, and I say this as someone who has switched three times and would rather not do it again. Pick whichever one you like; the acquisition is coming for that one too. What you actually want is the property that ownership can't reach: a client you can compile, a vault format someone else can read, an export that includes the seeds, a codebase that can be forked by people who are angry. Not because open source is virtuous. Because a fork is the only exit that doesn't depend on the goodwill of whoever bought your password manager this morning:

https://example.com/vault-formats

The industry sold you the vault and then sold the vault. Anything you can't fork, you're renting from the next guy.
