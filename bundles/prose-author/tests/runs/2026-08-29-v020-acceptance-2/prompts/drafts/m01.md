### Observation instructions

- o01 [person-reader-stance; section:address; measurement:second-person-family]: Direct address turns policy analysis into practical civic action or asks readers to test a bill against familiar online behavior. Reserve it for concrete scenarios and closing participation prompts; keep the explanatory body focused on affected groups, institutions, and consequences rather than maintaining a conversational address across the supported contexts.
- o02 [person-reader-stance, self-reference-biography; section:address; measurement:first-person-plural-family]: The plural voice usually speaks for the advocacy organization—its analysis, outreach, litigation perspective, or recommended policy—while occasionally widening to a public exposed to shared risks. Use it to claim institutional judgment or collective stakes, and identify the organization when the referent might otherwise blur.
- o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contracted forms keep legal and technical analysis readable and give rebuttals spoken force, especially when exposing the gap between statutory assurances and likely effects. Use them in plain-language explanations, pivots, and emphatic verdicts, while allowing quoted legislation and formal descriptions to retain their original diction.
- o04 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negatives sharpen categorical limits: what a bill fails to require, what existing law does not permit, or what institutions should not do. Place them where precision or emphasis matters, particularly in legal distinctions and concluding recommendations; use conversational contractions for quicker rebuttal elsewhere.
- o05 [self-reference-biography; section:absences; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: The individual writer recedes behind institutional analysis, documented evidence, and collective advocacy. Build authority through the organization’s actions and the public consequences of policy rather than personal experience or opinion; when a speaking subject is needed, prefer the institutional plural established by the companion measured form.
- o06 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions dramatize statutory contradictions by walking through plausible cases, or they frame the inquiry that the following analysis answers. Use a compact cluster when testing how broad rules affect ordinary speech and reserve isolated questions for a central policy uncertainty; recommendations themselves are usually declarative imperatives.
- o07 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses supply compact legal names, acronyms, age definitions, examples, and clarifications without diverting the main causal argument. Use them for information a general reader needs to decode the policy, keeping the surrounding sentence intelligible on its own and avoiding extended argumentative detours inside the aside.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:em-dashes]: Em dashes stage a forceful qualification, consequence, or compressed example after a proposition has been established. They often make an abstract legal mechanism concrete or expose the practical meaning behind official wording. Place them at pivots within sentences, but let ordinary syntax carry longer chains of evidence.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:en-dashes]: Do not rely on en dashes as the characteristic interruption mark. Use the established em dash for pivots and parentheses for compact clarification; retain an en dash only when source typography or a conventional range calls for it, rather than introducing it as a competing rhetorical signal.
- o10 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Concessions acknowledge a legitimate goal, a narrowed amendment, or a real underlying harm before arguing that the proposed mechanism remains overbroad or counterproductive. Predictions are tied to incentives with modals such as “may,” “could,” and “likely.” Place qualification immediately before the pivot to practical consequences.
- o11 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Name lawmakers, agencies, companies, coalitions, and courts according to their role, then test their claims against bill text, legal history, or operational incentives. Credit allies and linked sources explicitly; quote opponents’ terms briefly before correcting their implications. Keep criticism aimed at proposals, institutions, and documented conduct.
- o12 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures draw from familiar infrastructure and risk: a card catalog instead of a database, a valuable vault of personal records, a dragnet, or a movie whose ending is already known. Use a short concrete comparison after technical explanation to expose scale, incentives, or foreseeable harm; do not sustain it beyond the point.
- o13 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Open with the current legislative event or a blunt public-interest premise, then state the hidden consequence within the opening movement. Section endings often compress the causal result into a short verdict. Close by returning to the governing value or core harm and issuing a specific institutional or reader-facing demand.
