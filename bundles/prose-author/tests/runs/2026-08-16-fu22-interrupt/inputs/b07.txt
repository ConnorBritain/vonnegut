You don't own the movies in your library.

I know how that sounds: you clicked a button marked "Buy", you paid more than the rental price precisely because it was a purchase, the receipt in your inbox says purchase, and the film sat for years on a shelf the app itself calls "Your Library" — and then one Tuesday it wasn't there, and nobody wrote to tell you, and there was no refund, because as far as every other party to that transaction is concerned, nothing was taken from you.

Here's what happened: a contract between a studio and a platform ran out, nobody renewed it, and the platform's permission to show you that film ended in the same instant the contract did. The film didn't move. Your money didn't come back either, because the money was never for the film. It was for what the industry calls "a licence to access": revocable, terminable, and priced as though it were a thing you could put on a shelf.

Picture the appliance shop on the high street, the one with a counter and a till and a kid who wheels the box out to your car. You buy a chest freezer. Some months later two men in a van pull up at your kitchen, unplug it, carry it out, and leave a summer's worth of meat sweating on the floor, and when you call the shop the manager explains, patiently, that the wholesaler's arrangement with the manufacturer has lapsed, that this is a routine matter of "catalogue availability", and that you never bought a freezer at all: you bought the ongoing ability to keep things cold, which is a service, and services end.

You'd call the police, and the police would come, because in the physical world the men in the van are thieves and everyone in the story knows it. The only reason the streaming version doesn't look like theft is that nobody has to come to your kitchen — the freezer was always in their van, and letting you look at it was the entire product. The lawyers who drew that distinction pulled it out of somewhere dark and billed by the hour for the retrieval. It's bullshit.

This isn't hypothetical and it isn't new: Amazon reached into people's Kindles in 2009 and deleted copies of 1984 that those people had paid for, which is a coincidence you couldn't get past an editor. Sony told PlayStation customers in 2023 that Discovery shows they'd purchased were going away, then walked it back once the noise got loud enough. Ubisoft shut down The Crew in 2024 and the game people had bought stopped launching at all. I've had two films go missing out of my own library, and I can't tell you whose contract expired, because nobody is obliged to tell me. Every one of these ends in the same shrug, and the shrug is the point: the terms said this could happen, you agreed to the terms, and there is no line item anywhere in the world for what you lost.

What makes it stick is that you can't keep a copy. The file on your device is wrapped in DRM, and in the US, breaking that wrapper is a felony under Section 1201 of the DMCA even when the thing you're doing with it is legal. That's the trick. It isn't that the platform owns your library. It's that the law makes it a crime for you to hold anything the platform can't reach back and take.

We can at least fix the labelling, and California has started: a state law effective in 2025 bars sellers from advertising revocable digital goods with the words "buy" or "purchase" unless they disclose, right there at the point of sale, what you're actually getting. That's worth something. It is also the smallest available remedy, because a truthful label on a bad deal leaves you with a bad deal, and what we want is the thing we used to have with discs and books and tapes: the right to keep what we paid for, offline, on hardware we control, in a format that outlives the company that sold it to us.

So check what you actually have. If a film matters to you, get it on a disc, or from one of the few shops that will still sell you an unlocked file, and treat the tile in your library as a rental with an unusually long fuse. The button will go on saying Buy. It will go on meaning rent. The only party to that transaction who ever thought otherwise was you.
