Overdraft fees are a poverty tax, and the people who pay it never agreed to pay it.

Start with who pays, because the industry line has always been that this is a small grace extended to the occasionally forgetful — the customer who misjudged his balance on a Tuesday and would rather his gas bill went through than bounced. The CFPB's own numbers tell a different story: fewer than a tenth of checking accounts generate the overwhelming majority of all overdraft revenue, and the people holding those accounts don't overdraw once a year, they overdraw a dozen times, thirty times, forty times, because there is no buffer in the account and there never was one. This isn't a fee on carelessness. It's a fee on being broke, charged at the exact moment being broke is most expensive — the Gospel of Matthew run as a business model, unto him that hath shall be given, and from him that hath not shall be taken away. At the peak, the banks were pulling something like $15bn a year out of precisely those accounts.

Now the part where a fee stops being a fee and becomes a machine: the order.

Picture a supermarket. You've got $50 in your pocket and a cart holding a $38 turkey and eleven small things — bread, milk, apples, a toothbrush — and the cashier gets to ring them up in whatever sequence he likes. Ring the small stuff first and you clear eleven items and stall on the turkey: one thing you can't afford, one problem. Ring the turkey first and you're broke by item two, and everything after it fails. Same cart. Same $50. The only variable is the sequence, and the sequence belongs to the guy at the till.

That's high-to-low posting, and for years it was ordinary practice at the largest banks in the country. The debits hitting your account on a given day didn't post in the order you spent them, they posted biggest first, which is the arrangement that maximises the number of small transactions stranded below zero, and each stranded transaction carries its own $35. Your rent overdrew you. Your coffee got charged for it, along with the parking meter, the pharmacy, and the thing you bought your kid at lunchtime.

The banks had a story about this, and I want you to sit with how insulting it is. High-to-low, they said, reflected "customer preference": surely you'd want the mortgage to clear before the latte. It's bullshit, and it was bullshit that somebody was paid to pull out of his ass and put in a compliance binder. Judge Alsup, who tried the Wells Fargo case in California, read the internal documents and found what everyone in the building already knew, which is that the reordering was built for fee income and the rationale was assembled afterwards. He ordered $203m returned to the people it had been taken from.

So why did a practice this well-documented last as long as it did? Because the market-discipline story requires an exit and there isn't one. If you're overdrawing forty times a year you are not a customer a rival bank is competing for, you're a customer the rival bank would also like to charge $35, and if you leave in the wrong way — close an account while it's still negative — you land in ChexSystems and can find yourself shut out of retail banking altogether, paying a cheque casher three percent for the privilege of touching your own wages.

And it lasted because we kept mistaking paperwork for a fix. The 2010 rules made banks get your opt-in before covering a debit-card or ATM overdraft, which sounds like consent right up until you see how it was sold: "overdraft protection", a courtesy, the bank stepping in to spare you embarrassment at the register. Cheques and automatic payments were never covered by the opt-in at all. Then the CFPB wrote a rule capping the fee at $5 at the biggest banks, and Congress killed it under the Congressional Review Act, which doesn't merely repeal a rule, it bars the agency from writing a substantially similar one. I think that last part explains the lifespan better than anything else does. What got repealed wasn't the cap; it was the permission to try again.

The banks had a word for the state your account was in when the fees landed: insufficient. There was money in it. There were goods legitimately paid for. And there was somebody choosing the sequence and collecting $35 every time the sequence ran out. The funds were never insufficient. The order was.
