The theory of open standards goes like this. A room full of rivals who can't stand each other sit down and agree on the shape of a plug, the order of the bytes, what a date looks like, how one machine says hello to another — and then they go back to their corners and compete on the things that actually matter, like price and speed and whether the thing still works on a Sunday.

Nobody owns the plug. Anyone can make one. You can take your files, your customers and your fifteen years of email and walk out on a Tuesday, and a vendor who knows you can walk out on Tuesday treats you better on Monday. It's a genuinely beautiful idea, and it's the last wholly true thing most of us ever hear about standards.

What happens next has happened every time. The biggest implementer in the room — the one whose browser your bank tested its website against, the one whose file format your legal department has typed into for twenty years, the one holding the calendar of every person you need in a meeting — ships the standard, and then ships a little more than the standard. An extra field. A helpful header. An (entirely optional, entirely voluntary) flag that makes everything faster when both ends understand it. Both ends never do.

If the extra bit is so good, why not put it in the spec where the other implementers can have it? You already know. Ship the standard and you're a good citizen. Ship the standard plus one field and you're an innovator. Ship the standard plus one field that only your own servers understand, and after a while you're the standard. The extra bit isn't valuable because it's good; it's valuable because they have it and you don't, and every month it stays outside the spec is another month in which the compatible option is the shittier option for the people you're trying to sell to.

Think about the potluck on your street. The rule everyone agreed to is boring and load-bearing: bring your dish in a nine-by-thirteen pan, because a nine-by-thirteen fits every oven on the block, every fridge shelf and everybody's arms.

Then the neighbor with the big kitchen, the one who hosts most often, starts bringing pans with a little lip on the rim. The lip is great. The lid seals, nothing sloshes in the car, and he isn't stopping you from bringing your flat-rimmed pan — he'd never, the rule is the rule. He just also hands out his lids for free, and a year later every drawer on the street is full of lids that seal on his pans and no others, and yours is the dish that turns up swimming in its own juice.

Now he charges for the lids. Now he hosts every week, because his oven is the one everything fits, and that is the whole fucking point of the lip. Nobody voted, nobody defected, nobody broke the agreement; everybody just kept picking the pan that didn't leak. That isn't an illustration of lock-in. That's the mechanism, shrunk down small enough to hold.

Microsoft named this move in its own internal memos — embrace, extend, extinguish — and then had the bad luck of hearing those memos read back to it in public, first in the Halloween Documents that Eric Raymond published in 1998 and later at an antitrust trial. Having been caught naming it, they went ahead and did it. Windows 2000 shipped Kerberos with an extra field in it, and the document explaining that field arrived wrapped in a license you had to click through to read. Free Kerberos servers spoke Kerberos. Microsoft's spoke Kerberos and one word more, and guess which dialect your domain controller had to answer in. They'd run the same play on Java, which is why Sun sued them in 1997.

The web got the treatment later, in a friendlier font. AMP was HTML with Google's extensions bolted on, and serving your pages out of Google's cache was what got you into the carousel at the top of the results — an offer nobody was forced to take and everybody with a traffic target took.

I think what makes the extension such a durable weapon is that it never once requires anyone to say the quiet part. Lawrence Lessig told us that code is law, and the lid-sellers have been legislating ever since: not in the spec, where you could object, but in the release notes, where you can only comply. Ask why the interoperable path is second-class and you'll get a shrug, a bug tracker and a reminder that you were always free not to use it, which is how a standards body says fuck off.

So consider the word. An extension is what you build onto a house that stands up fine without it — a wing off the back, a sunroom, a thing you could knock down on a bad Saturday and still have somewhere to sleep. Go look at what your organization would actually lose by switching vendors on Friday, and count how much of it is spec and how much of it is extension. The extension is the load-bearing wall now, and it belongs to somebody who isn't you.
