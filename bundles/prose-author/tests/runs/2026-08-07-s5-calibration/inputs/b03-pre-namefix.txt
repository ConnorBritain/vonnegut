Parts pairing is the worst thing that has ever happened to your stuff. Not the glue, not the screws with the weird five-lobed heads, not the service manual they won't sell you at any price — the thing where you buy the correct part, the real part, the part that rolled off the same line in the same factory and sat in the same bin as the one currently dying inside your device, and the device looks at it and refuses it. The part is genuine. The device is lying to you about that.

The lie has a shape, and the shape is a number. There's a serial number burned into the chip in your screen, and a matching number remembered by the mainboard, and the two of them were married at the factory in a ceremony you weren't invited to. Swap the screen and the numbers disagree. Nothing is broken. Nothing is counterfeit. You have done the repair correctly, with the right component, and the machine has decided to sulk about it, and there is no torque driver in the world that fixes a sulk.

That's why this is worse than glue, and I don't think the difference gets said plainly enough. Glue is a physical obstacle, and physical obstacles lose. A heat gun beats glue. Patience beats glue. Some kid with steady hands and a suction cup beats glue, and every year the kid gets better at it while the glue stays exactly as good as it was. Parts pairing doesn't lose to skill, because it isn't a barrier in the object — it's a permission held somewhere else, by someone who is not in the room and does not answer the phone.

Think about what that would look like if a lock did it. You lose your house key, so you walk down to the hardware store, and the guy puts a blank on the machine and cuts you a new brass key with teeth identical to the old one, down to the thousandth of an inch.

You walk home. You put the key in the door. The teeth line up, the pins lift, the cylinder turns — and then the lock checks a number stamped inside the blank, decides it isn't the number the builder wrote down when the house went up, and freezes solid. Your key is a real key. Your lock is a real lock. Your house is not your house.

Now the builder tells you there's a fix. He'll send a man out, and the man will bring an identical key from an identical machine, and he'll charge you four times the hardware store's price plus the callout, and afterwards you'll still own a door that phones him every time it's opened. That's a (very, very expensive) handshake, and you didn't get a vote on it. You never bought a lock. You bought a subscription to being let in.

That's the whole exercise, and once you see it, you can't unsee it in anything else. Solder on a new charging port and it still won't boot right. Buy the assembly used from an identical dead unit and it still won't boot right. Buy the part new, from them, at their price, and it still won't boot right until a technician with the correct login says the magic words over it.

So the question the pairing people can never answer: if the part is authentic, what exactly is being authenticated? Not the hardware. The hardware passed. What's being checked is whether the transaction went through their register, and calling that "security" is a lie told with a straight face by a room full of adults who know better. These serial-number bouncers have spent a decade insisting they're protecting you from fake components, while the thing they actually detect is a customer who didn't pay the toll. It's not security. It's a fucking turnstile with a security guard's jacket on.

And it's why the repair laws mostly haven't landed. Those laws were written against glue: sell the parts, sell the tools, sell the manual, and the repair follows. But you can hand somebody every part, every tool and every schematic and still hold the one number that makes them work, and the law goes home satisfied while the shop stays shut.

Genuine parts, they call it. The parts were always genuine. The only counterfeit in this transaction is the ownership they sold you.
