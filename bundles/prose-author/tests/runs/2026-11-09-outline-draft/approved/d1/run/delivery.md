The starter is the bakery. Everything a customer sees, from the crust to the open crumb to the loaf on the counter, is decided by a jar of flour and water that nobody photographs. The slow, invisible part of the work decides the result, long before anyone sees it.

Most teams have this backwards. They reward the part of the work that shows up in a demo, a dashboard or a Friday update. That's the shaping. Meanwhile they starve the part that decides whether the shaping will matter: the setup, the groundwork, the boring maintenance that has to happen every day whether or not anyone is watching.

The bakery's first month runs against that instinct. New people don't shape loaves. They don't score them or bake them. They feed the starter, and for a month that's all they do. No shaping, only feeding.

If you lead a team, notice how strange that sounds to you. A month without visible output feels like a month wasted. You'd want to see something. You'd want a loaf. That want is the problem. It pulls attention and praise toward the end of the process, where the result gets shown, and away from the beginning, where the result gets made.

Here's why it matters. When bread fails, it usually fails hours before anyone touches the dough. The starter was sluggish, or the fermentation ran short. By the time the loaf comes out flat and dense, the cause is long gone. It's sitting in a jar from the day before.

You can't rescue bad fermentation with good scoring.

Your team works the same way. The failure you see in the launch, the missed quarter or the brittle release was set in motion weeks earlier, in work nobody reviewed because it didn't look like anything. So the post-mortem blames the last visible step. The person who shaped the loaf takes the heat, and the jar sits there, unexamined, ready to do it again.

Two things fix this, and the bakery does both.

The first is the month. Give new people a month on the starter. Don't treat it as a test, or as a waiting room before the real job. It is the real job. A month of feeding teaches things nothing else can: how the jar smells when it's ready and when it's tired, how it behaves from one morning to the next, what happens when a feeding gets skipped. By the end, a new baker has a feel for the one part of the process that decides all the others. They've spent a month learning to care about something nobody will ever compliment.

Now apply that to your team. Your starter might be the data pipeline, the test suite, the customer research or the onboarding docs. Whatever it is, put new people on it first and leave them there long enough to understand it. A week of shadowing isn't enough. Leave them there until they start to notice when something is off.

The second thing is the notebook. The bakery has kept them for eleven years. The notebooks record what happened to the starter and the dough: when it was fed, how it behaved, how the bread came out. Most entries are dull, and that's the point. When a batch fails, the notebook lets you read the failure back. You can turn to the page from the day before and see what changed. Without it you're guessing, and a guess usually lands on whatever step is easiest to see.

Eleven years of notebooks isn't a record of brilliance. It's a record of attention, kept long enough to become useful.

Your team needs the same thing, and the people doing the slow work should be the ones who keep it. This isn't a status report written for leadership. It's a working log of the groundwork: what was changed, what was noticed, what looked a little wrong. When something breaks downstream, that log lets you trace it back upstream to the cause, instead of punishing the last pair of hands it passed through.

The month and the notebook work together. The month teaches people to pay attention to the invisible part. The notebook makes that attention outlast any one person's memory. Protect both. If the log gets skipped when things are busy, or the month gets cut short because you need hands on the visible work, you're trading the result for the look of progress.

There's an honest objection, and it comes from the people doing the feeding. To them, it looks like hazing.

The bakery had an early-shift baker who hated the first month. Feeding a jar while other people made bread felt like being kept out on purpose. From the inside, a month of groundwork can look like a gate the old hands put up so newcomers suffer the way they did.

Take that seriously. If you can't explain why the slow work matters, it is hazing. So explain it. Show them the notebook. Show them a failed batch and trace it back. Whether it's hazing or training comes down to whether the person understands what the work is for.

And then, at some point, something shifts.

The moment you know someone will be good is when they notice the starter. Nobody had to tell them. It's raining, and they adjust the water on their own. They've learned that the jar responds to the day it's in, and that tomorrow's bread depends on what they do with it today.

That's what you're waiting for on your team: the person who notices the groundwork before anything has gone wrong. You won't see it in a demo. It won't show up in the weekly update. It looks like a small change to the water on a rainy day.

Protect the month that makes it possible.
