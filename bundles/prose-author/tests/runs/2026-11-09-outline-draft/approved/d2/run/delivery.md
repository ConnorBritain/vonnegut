Every bakery has two kinds of work. There's the work people see: the shaping, the scoring, the loaves cooling on the rack with their tops split open. And there's the work nobody sees, which is the starter, a jar of flour and water that has to be fed and watched and kept alive before any of the visible work can happen.

The bakery starts new people in an odd place. Their first month has no shaping in it. None. They feed the starter. That's the job.

Most teams run the opposite way. They reward the part of the work that photographs well: the demo, the launch, the shipped feature, the slide with the line going up. Those things are real, and they deserve credit. But they're the scoring. Whether they work was decided earlier, in some jar nobody walks past. Someone cleaned up the data model. Someone kept the test suite honest. Someone sat through the boring conversation that settled a requirement before anyone wrote code. When you reward only the visible part, you're telling people where to spend their attention. They listen. The starter goes hungry, and for a while nothing looks wrong.

That's the trap. The failure doesn't happen where you're looking. With bread, it happens hours before anyone touches the dough, in a fermentation that went wrong while nobody was watching. By the time the loaf comes out flat, the cause is long past, and the person standing nearest the failure is the one who shaped it. There's a line for this: "You can't rescue bad fermentation with good scoring." No amount of skill at the end makes up for what went wrong at the start. Teams work the same way. The late-stage heroics get noticed, the late-stage misses get blamed, and the slow decay upstream stays invisible because nobody was assigned to watch it.

So here's what I'd ask you to steal from the bakery. Two things.

First, give new people a month on the starter. Not a month of shadowing the impressive work. A month of feeding the thing everything else depends on. At the bakery, that month is deliberately unglamorous: you come in, you feed, you look, you feed again. What it builds isn't a skill anyone would put on a résumé. It's a relationship with the slow part. You learn what healthy looks like by seeing it every day, and you learn what's off by seeing it the day it changes. Someone who has spent a month with the starter never again thinks of it as a jar on a shelf.

On a team, the starter is whatever the visible work quietly rests on. Maybe it's the build pipeline, the support queue, the onboarding docs, the dataset everyone pulls from. Put the new person there first. Make it their job, not their penance, and tell them why: this is the part that decides the rest, and you want them to know it from the inside before they ever touch the part people clap for.

Second, keep the notebook. The bakery has eleven years of them. Feedings, batches, odd days, all written down. That sounds like record-keeping for its own sake until a loaf fails. Then the notebook is the only way to read the failure back: to walk upstream from the flat bread on the rack to the morning when something in the jar was different. Without it, you're guessing, and the guess usually lands on whoever was closest to the end.

Most teams don't keep this kind of notebook. They keep tickets for the visible work and memory for everything else, and memory is exactly the thing that fails when you need it. A notebook doesn't have to be elaborate. It has to be kept, every day, by the people doing the slow work, and it has to be read when something goes wrong. Nobody gets to eleven years of notebooks by being inspired. You get there by writing tomorrow's entry, and then the one after that.

The two pieces depend on each other. The month teaches someone to notice. The notebook makes what they noticed outlast the moment they noticed it, and outlast them, too, if it comes to that. A team with only the month has people with good instincts and no record. A team with only the notebook has a record nobody knows how to read. You want both.

I'll be honest about the cost. To the person doing it, the month can look like hazing. The bakery's early-shift baker hated the first month. Hated it. If you do this on your team, some people will feel the same way, and they won't be entirely wrong to: a month of unglamorous work with no visible output feels like being made to pay dues. The difference between hazing and training is whether the work matters and whether you say so. Hazing is pointless on purpose. This isn't. So say it plainly, check in often, and don't pretend the month is fun. Let the people doing it be annoyed. Just make sure they know what they're building.

Here's how you'll know it worked. At the bakery, the moment you know someone is going to be good isn't when they shape a beautiful loaf. It's when they notice the starter: when it's raining and they adjust the water, because they've learned the jar answers to the weather. Nobody sees that adjustment. It won't show up in anything you'd put in a review. But it's the whole job, done right, long before anyone sees the result.

Watch for that moment on your team. Then protect the work that made it possible.
