Ownership is control, or it’s theater. When a maker can disable features after sale, you possess the device but share authority over what it may do. The maker has kept a hand on your controls – a hand you can’t remove, bargain with, or vote out. Call that arrangement whatever you like, but it is not full ownership.

Imagine buying a workshop from someone who retains the right to enter at night and weld shut whichever machines no longer suit the seller’s plans. You’ve got the deed, the keys, and the bill (definitely the bill), but the seller has kept the decisive power. Would you say you owned the whole workshop? You wouldn’t, because ownership that survives only at another party’s pleasure is tenancy dressed up for the checkout screen.

The confusion comes from treating possession as though it settled the question. It doesn’t. You can hold an object while someone else retains a veto over its useful functions (a veto hidden inside the thing itself). That veto is part of the bargain’s substance, no matter how polished the packaging or how permanent the word “buy” looks beside the price.

I think this distinction will matter more as devices become bundles of physical parts and revocable functions. But I don’t think complexity changes the moral arithmetic: if you paid for a capability, the maker shouldn’t get to repossess that capability while leaving the shell on your desk. That’s a repo man who only steals verbs – the object remains, but what it does has vanished. It’s an ingenious piece of bullshit because the loss can be made to look like a mere change in settings.

What exactly did you buy: the device, a temporary menu of permissions, or a lottery ticket whose prize changes after the draw? The honest answer can’t depend on which description flatters the maker. If the maker’s continuing consent is necessary for a feature to remain available, then that consent is part of the product (the invisible part with the sharpest teeth). You haven’t bought autonomy; you’ve bought supervised use.

Policy should start by refusing the damn euphemisms. A sale shouldn’t quietly become a lease whenever remote control benefits the seller, and “licensed feature” shouldn’t erase the ordinary meaning of buying the device that performs it. If makers want continuing control, they should disclose that bargain plainly and price it as continuing control. They shouldn’t collect the moral prestige of a sale while reserving the practical privileges of a landlord.

We also need a simple rule: disabling a paid feature after sale requires the owner’s authorization, except where the owner agreed to a genuinely temporary service. The agreement must be specific, comprehensible, and separable from the device purchase – not a scavenger hunt through contractual fog. Where the maker keeps unilateral power, we should treat the transaction as conditional access and regulate its advertising, refunds, and remedies accordingly. You don’t get to sell the cow and keep a switch that turns off the milk.

I think makers will argue that such limits reduce flexibility. Whose flexibility? Not yours: your device is the thing being bent, and you’re the person expected to accept that bending as innovation. We shouldn’t confuse a company’s freedom to alter your purchase with your freedom to use what you bought, because those freedoms point in opposite directions.

We can permit subscriptions, rentals, trials, and services without letting every sale become a fucking séance in which ownership appears at payment and disappears when the maker whispers a command. We only have to insist that the label match the power. If someone else can still decide which paid functions your device may perform, you’re holding their property arrangement, not exercising full ownership of yours.

A maker’s post-sale kill switch doesn’t merely modify the product; it exposes who remained in charge all along. Ownership with an off switch in somebody else’s hand isn’t ownership.
