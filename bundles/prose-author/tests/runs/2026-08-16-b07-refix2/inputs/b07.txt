The word "buy" on a streaming service is a lie. You clicked a button that said "Buy." You paid the buy price and not the rental price. You paid it with the same card, in the same session, on the same screen where you rent things for three dollars, and the film turned up on a shelf the company calls "Your Library." Then one morning it isn't there. No email, no refund, no tombstone where the file used to be – just a gap in the grid and a help page explaining that content availability is subject to change.

Here's what happened, and none of it happened to you. Somewhere a studio's licensing arm and a platform's content team signed a contract with an end-date on it (they always have an end-date; perpetuity is expensive, and nobody pays perpetuity money for a mid-tier back-catalogue title). The date came. Nobody renewed, because the renewal price went up, or because the rights-holder launched a service of its own and wants that title as a moat, or because the catalogue got sold sideways in an acquisition to somebody with different plans. And then the platform did the one thing its architecture makes easy and its lawyers make cheap: it reached into a shelf in your house and took the thing back.

That reach is the entire story.

It's worth being exact about why the reach is possible, because the answer isn't that computers are hard. You never had a file. You had a licence to decrypt a file, and the key that does the decrypting sits on a company-owned server you will never touch, run by people whose interests stopped aligning with yours the second the money cleared. That's DRM, and DRM is not a lock you're allowed to pick: Section 1201 of the DMCA makes breaking it a potential felony even when the thing behind the lock is something you paid for outright. So the escape hatch that exists for every other durable good you have ever bought (repair it, back it up, move it somewhere the seller can't follow) is the one hatch that's specifically illegal here.

You feel this one in your gut before you can argue it, and the gut is right. Every other purchase you make comes with a quiet assumption that the seller then leaves. The hardware store does not keep a key to your garage in case the hammer's manufacturer gets acquired. But a digital purchase is a sale in which the seller never leaves, keeps a key, and reserves the right to let itself back in – and the industry's word for that arrangement, on the rare occasions it is forced to have one, is "flexibility." It isn't flexibility. I've bought films this way for fifteen years and I could not tell you which of them are still in my account tonight, which is what makes the whole arrangement such bullshit.

So why does the button still say "Buy"? Because "Buy" converts and "a revocable licence, terminable at our sole discretion" does not. Why is there no refund when the licence lapses? Because you were never sold the film – you were sold a service, and the service is technically still running. It's just running without the film in it.

There are fixes and not one of them is exotic. California decided that a store can't put the word "buy" on a thing it can take back, which is a small law making a large point: the word on the button has to describe the transaction underneath it. But we can go further than the button. We can insist that when a licence lapses on something sold as a purchase, the seller either refunds our money or hands over a copy that phones home to nobody (a file without a leash is not a moral hazard; it is what a purchase used to mean). And we can stop pretending that a law criminalising the removal of a lock from our own property was ever a copyright measure.

Until then, do the unglamorous thing: treat anything with a Buy button next to a streaming logo as a long-term rental at a purchase price, and keep the things you actually love somewhere that a contract renegotiation two thousand miles away cannot reach. I have a shelf of discs I felt faintly embarrassed about for a decade. I don't any more. Your library isn't yours. It's their library, and they have been letting you look at it.
