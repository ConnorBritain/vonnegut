A device is not fully owned when its maker can disable features after sale. That may sound like a narrow dispute about software, but it goes to the basic meaning of ownership. When we buy something, we expect to decide how it works, when it works, and whether it remains useful after the transaction is over. If the maker retains a switch that can turn capabilities off, the buyer has received possession without receiving complete control.

The usual defense is that the hardware still sits in the buyer's home (the screen, the battery, the sensors, the casing), so ownership must be intact. That argument treats the device as a pile of parts rather than as the thing people actually purchased. A modern device is the hardware and the functions that make the hardware useful—and those functions can be withheld even after the money has changed hands. The distinction matters because a locked feature is not merely an inconvenience; it is a condition imposed on the buyer's continued use.

What exactly has been bought if the most important capabilities remain subject to someone else's permission? The answer cannot simply be that the buyer accepted the terms, because consent to a contract does not erase the difference between owning a tool and renting access to it. A person may agree to restrictions, but that agreement should not transform a sale into ownership in name only. The maker's control is the point—not a technical footnote attached after the fact.

This is especially troubling when the disabled feature was part of the reason the device was purchased in the first place. The buyer may have paid for a capability, built routines around it, and relied on it as part of the device's identity. If that capability can later disappear, the buyer carries the cost while the maker keeps the authority to redefine the product. That is less like buying a machine than receiving a key to a machine whose owner can change the lock.

The problem is not solved by calling the disabled feature an update, a service decision, or a change to the user experience. Those labels describe the maker's action, but they do not answer the ownership question. A feature does not become less central because it is delivered through code, and control does not become less real because it is exercised remotely. The software layer is still part of the product's practical operation—even if the sales language tries to place it somewhere else.

We should be careful about accepting a market where ownership ends at the moment a device leaves the store. That arrangement gives makers an ongoing veto over products they have already sold, while buyers are left with the bill and a shrinking set of choices. It may also encourage companies to design devices around dependency, because a customer who cannot preserve a purchased function is easier to convert into a continuing subscriber. The result is a marketplace that looks competitive on the shelf but behaves like a series of private permissions afterward.

Digital rights have always required us to look past the physical object and ask who actually controls it. That question is now unavoidable for ordinary devices, not just for specialized software or online services. If a maker can disable features after sale, the buyer should have meaningful rights to keep using what was purchased. Otherwise, the transaction transfers possession but withholds the authority that ownership is supposed to provide.

We should not let a company sell a device as a finished product and reserve the power to make it unfinished later. A sale should mean more than temporary access dressed up as ownership. When the maker can reach into a buyer's device and remove what made it worth buying, the buyer owns the object only in the thinnest sense. That is not full ownership, and policy should treat it accordingly.
