```markdown
"Ownership" is a word the industry's been quietly hollowing out for years, and I'd argue most of us haven't noticed because the hole keeps getting bigger one firmware update at a time. When you buy a device and the maker can reach back in and disable features after the sale, you don't own that device – you've leased it. You've purchased a conditional license to use it, at the manufacturer's ongoing pleasure, until you haven't.

Think about what it meant to buy a physical object before everything got a software layer. The seller hands it over, the transaction closes, and their claim on it ends: that's what a sale is. The dealer doesn't get to send a signal six months later that disables your heated seats because they've launched a subscription tier for climate control. If they tried that (and we all know they'd love to), we'd call it fraud, and we'd be right. But that's exactly the architecture that device makers have been quietly building into products for a decade, and because the mechanism is invisible – a patch that downloads at 3am while you're asleep – the outrage hasn't kept pace with reality.

Here's what's actually happening to your stuff. You pay full retail, take the thing home, and use it for a year, maybe two. Then the company decides the feature you factored into your purchase decision is now a premium add-on, and it vanishes from your unit in a silent update. And if you find out at all, it's in a change-log written for lawyers, not users. What would you call that if it happened to a refrigerator? You'd call it fraud, and you'd want the attorney general involved. But because it's software, we've somehow agreed to treat it as a terms-of-service question rather than a property question, which is exactly where the industry needs us to be.

John Deere has made the underlying logic explicit: the software running your tractor is licensed, not sold, so the company retains ongoing control over what you do with the machine you nominally own. Tesla has remotely modified battery range on vehicles that customers already paid for. I think these aren't outliers; they're the model the whole industry is working toward. The physical object transfers; the functional capability stays on a leash the manufacturer holds.

The "it's in the EULA" defense is where this gets genuinely dishonest. Yes, technically there's a clause (buried past forty screens of boilerplate, in type sized for discouragement rather than reading) that discloses some version of the arrangement. What kind of consent gets manufactured that way? That's not consent – it's a legal fig leaf, and we shouldn't let the industry use it to pull this shit with a straight face. A transaction where the seller retains ongoing unilateral control over the product's functionality is not a sale. It's a rental with a one-time upfront fee, and calling it a purchase is a lie.

I don't think this is hard to fix, in principle. We need mandatory disclosure at point of sale for any feature subject to post-purchase modification – not tucked in the EULA, but on the box, in plain language. We need right-to-repair statutes with real teeth that don't carve out the software layer (where all the control actually lives). And we need courts (I know, I know) to stop treating 6,000-word boilerplate agreements as evidence of informed consent. The word "sale" should mean something. Until legislatures decide it does, every "buy" button is a promise the seller never intends to keep.
```
