Men set out their intentions as merchants set out their wares, in the front of the shop and in the best light; but the life is made in the back room, where the small habit works unwatched. A man resolves to be temperate, and the resolution is a fine thing, entire and unbroken, requiring nothing of him until the hour it is tried; whereas the habit of rising at one hour rather than another, or of answering a letter the day it comes, has already spent itself a thousand times before any resolution is put to the proof. Therefore the intention is the debt a man means to pay, and the habit is the coin he pays with.

It is commonly held that great actions come of great purposes. Rather, they come of small ones long repeated, which have made the great action easy at the moment it was required. The man who has practised plain speaking in trifles will speak plainly when it costs him something; and the man who has flattered in trifles will flatter then also, and will call it prudence.

Habits are like the small tools of a trade, which are taken up so often that the hand shapes itself to them; therefore let a man look well to what he handles daily, for he will be shaped by it whether he consents or no. A great instrument is used seldom and put away; the small one is never put away at all.

There is this further difference, that intentions are known to the man himself and habits are known to his household. He may examine his purposes and find them good, and be honestly satisfied; but his servants and his wife could tell in a quarter of an hour what he is, having watched what he does when he is not attending to himself. Certainly a man is less what he has resolved than what he has repeated, and the second is legible where the first is not.

Yet it is not to be said that intention is nothing. It is the thing that plants the habit; but it plants only, and does not grow it, and the planting is the least part of the labour. Many a man has planted well and lost the whole crop for want of returning to the ground.

The correction of a life, then, goes not by great vows but by small alterations kept up. A vow undertakes to make a man over at once, and is broken at once, and the breaking discourages him from the lesser thing that would have served. Let him rather change one small practice, and keep it until it is no longer a change; and then another. This is slow, and slowness is the reason it is despised, and the reason it works. For nature is not overthrown by assault; she is quietly taught, as a horse is taught, by the same small usage every day, until she goes the way she is put without knowing she has been turned.

And let him not think the matter indifferent because the particulars are small. The sea is made of drops, and the ship is lost by a seam.
