Eleven years, and what I have to show for it is a shelf: forty-one notebooks, most of them soft-covered, some of them water-damaged, one of them with a coffee ring on the inside back cover that I remember making. I stopped in March. Nobody asked me to stop and nothing happened to make me.

The reason I gave people at first was that I had run out of things worth writing down. That was a lie of the flattering kind. I had not run out. I went back through the last three notebooks with the honest intention of finding out what I had been doing, and here is what is in them: overheard lines on the bus, prices, the names of trees I looked up and have since forgotten again, a description of a man's coat, four beginnings, a list of things to say to my brother, weather. Nothing was missing. The trouble was that I had begun writing for the notebook.

You know how that goes. A thing happens in front of you and instead of standing there in it you are already choosing the verb. I was on a train in February and a woman across the aisle fell asleep with her mouth open and her hand still holding her phone upright, like a soldier who has died on watch and stayed standing, and I was pleased with myself before she had finished falling asleep. Then I wrote it down. Then the rest of the ride was spent guarding the sentence so it would survive to the platform. I got a good line. I did not get the train.

That is the whole of it. The notebook stopped being a place I put things and became the reason I went out.

There is a second thing, less respectable. The notebook let me believe I was working. Four days of a bad week could go by with nothing done and the notebook would have six entries in it, and six entries feel like six days. The ledger was full and the account was empty. I suspect this is why so many of us keep them. Ask yourself what your last month's pages actually became. Did any of it turn into a paragraph you kept?

I am not going to tell you the notebook is a vice. Half the writers I admire kept one and one of them kept his in a hand nobody but him could read, which strikes me now as the sensible arrangement. It is a tool, and a tool that has begun operating you is a thing you put down for a while.

What I do instead is worse and better. I remember badly. A month later what survives is the mouth, the phone, the posture, and whatever the memory has done to it in the meantime, which is generally more useful than what I would have written on the train. Memory throws out the trees and the prices. It keeps the coat.

Ask me in a year and I may have started again. Meanwhile my desk has room on it, and the shelf is somebody else's problem.

Don't send me your notebooks. I mean it — I'll read them and then we'll both be back where we started.
