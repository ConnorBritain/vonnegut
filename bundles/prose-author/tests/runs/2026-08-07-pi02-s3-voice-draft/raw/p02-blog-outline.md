Somewhere on your calendar there is a recurring meeting that died months ago and has not been buried. Thirty minutes, weekly, six people, a round of updates that every one of them could have typed in a sentence. Nobody says anything. It is on the calendar.

Cancel it first. Not the one-on-ones, not the planning session everyone complains about — that one. It is the cheapest thing you will ever do and it takes a click.

Why does a dead meeting keep breathing? Because each person in it believes somebody else needs it. Your senior engineer sits through it for the junior. The junior sits through it because the senior seems to want it. You keep it because you inherited it and cancelling a meeting in your first month looks like a man rearranging the furniture instead of doing the work. So six people protect a thing none of them wants, the way a family keeps a piano nobody plays.

Ask them. Not in the meeting — in writing, one at a time, and ask plainly: what do you get out of Tuesday at ten? You will get four answers that amount to nothing and one that amounts to something real. That one is the whole business.

What was real in it was usually one of three things: people found out what everyone else was doing, blockages surfaced, and somebody who never speaks up got a scheduled minute in which to speak up. Each of those has a cheaper form. Write down what you're doing and where you're stuck, in a place the others can read, by Tuesday morning. A blocked person says so the hour they are blocked, not on the third day, and says it to the person who can unblock them. The one who doesn't speak up gets a standing half-hour with you alone, which is the only place they were ever going to say the thing anyway.

That is three habits replacing one meeting, and two of them will not take. Start with the writing. It is the one that survives.

Now the case for keeping it. If the team is new — genuinely new, three weeks old, strangers who have not yet worked out who is competent at what — keep the meeting and keep it for a while. What looks like status is actually people learning each other's names, hearing each other be wrong about something, finding out that the quiet one knows the payments system cold. You cannot get that in a document. You cannot get it from me either. Keep it until you notice they've started talking to each other outside it, and then kill it.

You will read forty articles this month telling you what to do with your calendar, and mine is one of them. Take this much: the recurring meeting is the only decision you inherited that you can reverse in ten seconds and observe the results of within a week. Almost nothing else you do as a manager has that shape. Most of your decisions will take a quarter to show whether they were any good — the hire, the reorganisation, the thing you said in the review. This one reports back by Friday.

So cancel it and watch. If a week passes and nobody has asked where it went, you were right and you now have a small piece of evidence about your own judgement, which at this stage you have almost none of. If three people ask, put it back and say you were testing it. Nobody will mind. Nobody has ever minded about this.

Send the cancellation before you talk yourself out of it. Your calendar will look emptier than it did, and you will find that unpleasant for about a day.
