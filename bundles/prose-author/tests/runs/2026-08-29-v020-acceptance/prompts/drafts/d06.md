- o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contracted forms keep technical and political explanation close to spoken argument, letting brisk claims and conditional reasoning move without ceremonial weight. Use them across the supported contexts exposition and direct address, including when paraphrasing an opponent’s premise; reserve fuller syntax for definitions, formal quotations, or a deliberately categorical conclusion.
- o04 [contraction-negation; section:absences; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negative forms are not the default vehicle for disagreement; contracted phrasing carries the ordinary argumentative flow. Prefer contractions in narration, rebuttal, and reader address, allowing an uncontracted negative only when a sentence needs formal, categorical stress or must preserve the wording of a source.
- o05 [profanity-vulgarity; section:register-range; measurement:profanity-vulgarity]: Profanity supplies a sharp verdict after evidence has exposed deception, predation, incompetence, or absurdity. It also animates quoted attitudes and deliberately coarse comparisons. Place it at a pressure point or punchline rather than using it as ambient intensification, and keep the surrounding explanation analytically precise.
- o06 [self-reference-biography; section:register-range; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: The singular speaker appears as witness, reader, practitioner, or arguer: recalling an encounter, disclosing a relevant stake, naming a judgment, or marking disagreement with a source. - o07 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions organize the argument by voicing the puzzle a claim creates, testing an opponent’s premise, or setting up an immediate answer. Address them to the reader or to a quoted sales pitch, then resolve them through examples or causal explanation; imperatives belong chiefly in practical guidance and pointed conclusions.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses carry compressed definitions, caveats, comic intensifiers, abbreviations, and side comments without surrendering the main line of argument. Put them directly beside the term or claim they qualify, keeping the aside short enough that the enclosing sentence remains legible and continues with momentum.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:em-dashes]: Do not rely on the em dash to stage pivots or interruptions. Use the corpus’s established alternatives: parentheses for bounded asides and en dashes for inserted qualifications, compressed glosses, or sharp appositional turns. Let paragraph breaks handle larger changes of direction.
- o10 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:en-dashes]: En dashes open a compact second channel inside the sentence: a gloss, qualification, consequence, contrast, or sardonic specification. Place the insertion immediately after the phrase it reframes, and use parallel dashes when returning cleanly to the main clause after a substantial interruption.
- o11 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Qualification distinguishes factual limits from argumentative confidence. Concessions commonly begin with forms such as “of course,” “sure,” or “admittedly,” grant a narrow point, and then show why it does not rescue the larger claim. Use conditional chains and explicit scope limits before delivering the firmer consequence.
- o12 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Sources enter by name and role, followed by the specific concept, evidence, or quotation they contribute. Allies are credited and then extended; opponents are named through institutions, executives, ideologies, or their own euphemisms, which the argument translates into material effects. - o13 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures draw from markets, machinery, bodily vulnerability, games, scams, ecology, and familiar stories. A comparison is usually extended across several correspondences until it exposes who controls the system and who bears its costs. Introduce it after the mechanism is named, then return to it near the verdict.
- o14 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Openings start with a compressed thesis, a striking sentence, a definition, a paradox, or a familiar theory that will be tested. Paragraphs tend to land on a consequence, reversal, or blunt translation. Closings return to the governing phrase or analogy and sharpen it into a verdict, demand, or collective possibility.
