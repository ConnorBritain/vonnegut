- o01 [person-reader-stance; section:address; measurement:second-person-family]: Direct address shifts from policy explanation to practical consequence or action, casting the reader as a resident, user, or constituent affected by the proposal. - o03 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:contractions]: Contractions keep statutory and technical analysis accessible, especially in blunt rebuttals, causal explanations, and descriptions of what a bill permits or fails to do. Let them carry ordinary argumentative movement and plainspoken emphasis without turning formal source summaries into colloquial chatter.
- o04 [contraction-negation; section:cadence; measurement:uncontracted-negatives]: Full negatives give categorical legal, factual, or normative claims extra weight: a requirement does not solve the problem, a protection is not sufficient, or lawmakers should not proceed. Place them where the argument needs a firm boundary rather than merely a conversational rebuttal.
- o05 [self-reference-biography; section:absences; measurement:first-person-singular-family]: Individual self-reference yields to the institutional plural, so authority comes from organizational analysis, evidence, and public-interest commitments rather than personal experience. Frame judgments as the organization’s position or as claims supported by law and consequences, using the established plural voice instead of autobiographical authority.
- o06 [questions-imperatives-vocatives; section:address; measurement:question-marks]: Questions expose contradictions, test how a rule would operate, or enumerate lawful situations a broad proposal could sweep in. Address them to the policy logic rather than to a named opponent, then answer through immediate examples or analysis; they function as argumentative pressure, not invitations to dialogue.
- o07 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:round-parenthetical-spans]: Parentheses efficiently decode acronyms, supply legal examples, identify age ranges, or add a tightly bounded qualification without derailing the main claim. Use them for information that helps a general reader parse institutional detail, while leaving the sentence’s central causal line intact.
- o08 [interruption-punctuation; section:cadence; measurement:em-dashes]: Em dashes sharpen pivots between an official description and the practical reality, insert consequential examples, or land a corrective phrase after a complete claim. Use the interruption to make a hidden implication visible, especially when translating legislative language into effects on speech, privacy, or competition.
- o09 [interruption-punctuation; section:absences; measurement:en-dashes]: Use the established em dash or a parenthetical span for pivots, examples, and qualifications rather than relying on an en dash as an interruption mark. This keeps argumentative breaks visually consistent while preserving the prose’s preferred distinction between forceful correction and compact supplementary information.
- o10 [qualification-hedging; section:register-range]: Concessions acknowledge legitimate goals, real harms, or meaningful amendments before narrowing the dispute to means, definitions, and consequences. Predictions are tied to incentives with modals such as “may,” “could,” and “will likely”; place qualifications before a firmer bottom-line judgment rather than allowing them to blur it.
- o11 [opponents-allies-sources; section:address]: Name lawmakers, bill supporters, agencies, companies, and coalitions by role, then quote or summarize the precise claim under examination. Credit amendments and allied letters plainly, but correct opponents by testing their language against statutory mechanics, documented history, and foreseeable incentives rather than speculating about motives.
- o12 [figures-analogy; section:figures]: Figures draw from familiar systems and consequences: a card catalog contrasts with a database, regulation becomes a dragnet, stored conversations become a valuable vault, and premature legislation “jumps the gun.” Keep comparisons brief and explanatory, landing them where an abstract compliance or privacy problem needs physical clarity.
- o13 [openings-endings-closure; section:closings]: Open with the live legislative or technological development, identify its advertised purpose, then pivot quickly to the concealed practical problem. Paragraphs often close on the affected right or concrete consequence. End by restating the public-interest standard and issuing a specific demand to lawmakers, companies, or readers.
