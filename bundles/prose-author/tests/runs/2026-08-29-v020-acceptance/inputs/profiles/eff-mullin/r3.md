**Questions, imperatives, and vocatives.** Questions expose contradictions, test how a rule would operate, or enumerate lawful situations a broad proposal could sweep in. Address them to the policy logic rather than to a named opponent, then answer through immediate examples or analysis; they function as argumentative pressure, not invitations to dialogue.

## 5. Figures

**Figures and analogy vocabulary.** Figures draw from familiar systems and consequences: a card catalog contrasts with a database, regulation becomes a dragnet, stored conversations become a valuable vault, and premature legislation “jumps the gun.” Keep comparisons brief and explanatory, landing them where an abstract compliance or privacy problem needs physical clarity.

## 6. Register range

**Qualification and hedging.** Concessions acknowledge legitimate goals, real harms, or meaningful amendments before narrowing the dispute to means, definitions, and consequences. Predictions are tied to incentives with modals such as “may,” “could,” and “will likely”; place qualifications before a firmer bottom-line judgment rather than allowing them to blur it.
