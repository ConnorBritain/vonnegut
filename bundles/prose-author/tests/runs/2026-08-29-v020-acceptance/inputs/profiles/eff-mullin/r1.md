## 1. Cadence

**Contraction and negation.** Contractions keep explanations of statutes, technical systems, and predicted consequences conversational enough for a general readership. They work especially well in blunt pivots and plain-language glosses—turning legislative complexity into claims such as “it’s anything but.” Let them support clarity without making the register casual.

**Contraction and negation.** Full negative forms give legal limits, factual corrections, and recommendations extra weight: a bill “does not” solve a problem, lawmakers “should not” impose a rule, or evidence “cannot” support a claim. Place them where precision or categorical opposition matters more than conversational flow.

**Interruption punctuation.** Parentheses compactly identify bills and acronyms, define age ranges or legal standards, and insert concrete examples without derailing the claim. Use them for information that helps a general reader decode policy language; keep the principal consequence and argumentative turn in the main sentence.

**Interruption punctuation.** Em dashes sharpen pivots, attach an emphatic consequence, or interrupt a general claim with a decisive specification. They often make the hidden mechanism of a proposal visible: a protection is inverted, a burden reaches smaller actors, or a disclaimer fails. Place them at consequential turns rather than decorative pauses.

## 3. How a piece closes

**Openings, paragraph endings, and closure.** Openings name the live bill or development, summarize its advertised purpose, and pivot quickly to the central defect. Section endings compress the causal consequence into a short verdict. Final paragraphs turn analysis into a specific institutional demand—reject, reconsider, enforce existing law, or adopt the narrower alternative already established.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

**Person, number, and reader stance.** Direct address appears chiefly when converting analysis into a practical consequence or action: the reader is briefly placed inside a compliance burden, everyday online choice, or constituent role. Reserve “you” for concrete scenarios and closing participation rather than carrying the main institutional argument.

**Person, number, and reader stance.** The argument commonly speaks through an institutional “we”: EFF submits, believes, warns, contacts, or joins others. At times “us” broadens to everyone affected by a policy, but the reader is not automatically included. Use the plural to establish organizational standing or shared public exposure.

**Questions, imperatives, and vocatives.** Questions usually expose an operational gap by asking how a platform, family, or business could satisfy the proposal in practice. Short clusters can test several ordinary scenarios before the prose supplies its answer. Use them as structural pressure tests, not as requests for reader dialogue.

**Qualification and hedging.** Concessions precede opposition: transparency is laudable, a revised bill is narrower, parental concern is understandable, or the underlying harm is real. The prose then turns with “but,” “yet,” or “in practice” to show why the mechanism fails. Place concessions near the first major critique.

## 5. Figures

**Figures and analogy vocabulary.** Concrete figures translate infrastructure and liability into familiar objects or motion: a registry becomes a “card catalog,” stored conversations a “valuable vault,” and speech restrictions a “dragnet.” Extend the comparison only far enough to clarify scale, incentive, or danger, usually at the end of an explanatory passage.

## 6. Register range

**Named opponents, allies, and sources.** Bills, agencies, courts, coalitions, and named organizations enter through attributed claims, linked documents, or short quotations. Supporters are often treated as a position to test rather than personalized adversaries. Introduce external language precisely, then explain its practical effect or identify the gap between a disclaimer and its incentives.

## 7. What the corpus never does

Use the organizational plural for participation and conclusions, and ground personal-seeming evaluations in evidence or EFF’s stated position. Avoid autobiographical framing unless a particular firsthand role is indispensable to the policy analysis.
