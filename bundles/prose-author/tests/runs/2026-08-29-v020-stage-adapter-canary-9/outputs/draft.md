```markdown
The thing in your hand may be expensive, ingenious, and very nearly yours. But if its maker can reach through the network and switch off a feature you paid for, what you own is less like a tool than a rented room in somebody else’s building.

That distinction matters because ownership isn’t merely the right to possess an object while it behaves according to someone else’s permission. Ownership means you can use the thing, maintain it, modify it, and decide what it does within the bounds of law. You don’t need the maker’s continuing blessing every time you press a button. We understand this instinctively with a bicycle, a refrigerator, or a book. The manufacturer’s business ends at the sale, even if the warranty and support contract continue.

Connected devices complicate that old boundary. A phone, car, camera, or household appliance can contain software that performs a purchased function, while the maker retains a switch that can disable it. Sometimes the switch exists for safety or legal compliance (those cases deserve careful rules). Sometimes it exists because the company wants to change its product strategy. Those cases aren’t the same – we shouldn’t pretend they are.

The usual defense is that you agreed to the terms. Sure, you clicked through a license, and maybe the license says the feature can be withdrawn. But a contract can describe a relationship without making the relationship fair. If a shop sold you a lamp and reserved the right to remove its light whenever the shop changed its mind, you’d call that bullshit. The software version sounds more respectable only because the switch is invisible (and because the legal language is designed to make your eyes slide away).

What happens to your choice? You’ve paid the price, arranged your life around the function, and perhaps built other purchases around it. Then a server-side decision turns a working capability into a dead icon. You can’t repair the decision with a screwdriver, because the thing that broke isn’t sitting in your house. It’s sitting in a system you don’t control, operated by a firm that may be thousands of miles away.

I think this is the central policy question: when does a software-dependent feature become part of the owned device? My answer is that the label shouldn’t decide it. The practical test is what you were told you were buying, what you paid for, and whether the maker can later revoke it without your consent. A feature called a service, subscription, or enhancement can still be part of the bargain (especially when the advertising presents it as a reason to buy the device). What matters is the power to take it away.

We’re not arguing that every device must run forever under every circumstance. A dangerous defect may require a recall; a court order may require a change; a security failure may demand urgent action (though even then, the remedy should be narrow and explainable). But those exceptional powers should be bounded. They shouldn’t become a blank check for redesigning the bargain after the money has changed hands.

You should also be able to keep using a device when its maker abandons it. If a company shuts down the server that a product needs, the law should favor an offline mode, published interfaces, or tools that let you migrate. We can require documentation and interoperability, just as we require access to replacement parts in other markets. We can give users the right to inspect, repair, and preserve the software that makes a purchased feature work – including the tools needed to keep a lawful device useful.

The stakes aren’t only sentimental. A remote switch changes who holds power after the sale. It lets the maker convert a durable object into a continuing claim on your attention, money, and obedience. You may own the shell, but the company owns the conditions under which the shell remains useful. That’s possession with a leash (and a leash is still a form of control).

I don’t want a world where every update is treated as theft or every manufacturer is presumed malicious. I want a rule that makes the burden visible: if you sell a feature as part of a device, you can’t quietly reserve the right to take it back. We should write that rule into consumer law, repair law, and competition policy. Otherwise the next thing you buy may come with an invisible landlord – and you’ll be paying rent on something you already bought.
```
