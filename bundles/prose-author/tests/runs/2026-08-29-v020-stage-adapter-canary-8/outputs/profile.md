## 1. Cadence

**Contraction and negation.** Contractions keep the argumentative voice conversational and immediate, including in technically detailed explanations and direct rebuttals. They soften exposition without weakening its force, while clipped forms can accelerate a punchline or accusation. Prefer ordinary spoken contractions in the running argument, reserving more formal phrasing for deliberate emphasis.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

**Person, number, and reader stance.** Direct second-person address makes the reader a participant in the argument rather than a distant observer. It often frames a hypothetical, exposes a consequence, or turns an abstract dispute into a practical choice. Use “you” for pointed explanation and direct challenge, especially when translating systems into lived stakes.

## 6. Register range

**Profanity and vulgarity.** Profanity and vulgarity function as argumentative pressure, ridicule, and vivid compression rather than decorative slang. They surface in condemnations, comic analogies, and moments where bureaucratic language is exposed as evasive. Place a blunt term at the turn or payoff of a paragraph, and keep surrounding explanation precise.

**Self-reference and biography.** First-person singular places the writer in the argument as an interpreter, witness, or participant, not as the subject of autobiography. Personal memories and judgments introduce a framework or test its stakes before the prose returns to public analysis. Use “I” briefly to locate perspective, then resume the claim.

**Qualification and hedging.** Qualification usually appears as controlled concession rather than diffuse uncertainty: the prose grants a limited point, narrows its scope, then states the stronger conclusion. Phrases such as “of course,” “at least,” and “in other words” manage transitions between evidence and judgment. Place limits before the decisive turn.
