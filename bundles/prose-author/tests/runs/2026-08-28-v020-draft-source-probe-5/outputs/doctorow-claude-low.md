Ownership is a bundle of rights. The legal-theory framing, sure, but the practical test is straightforward: if someone else can reach into what you paid for and take pieces of it back, you didn't own it. You rented it with a purchase-shaped UI.

Think about what it means to buy a car. When you drive it off the lot, the dealer doesn't retain the right to disable your heated seats next quarter because they've decided that feature belongs in a subscription tier. The physical thing is yours. The title says so. The law (mostly) agrees. But when the thing in question is a phone, a tractor, a smart thermostat, or an insulin pump, the manufacturer has quietly inserted itself into the ownership chain in a way no car dealer ever could. They sold you hardware and held back the software that makes the hardware work. Features you paid for on Tuesday can be gone by Friday, and you have no recourse – you agreed to the terms. And because the terms of service run to forty pages nobody reads (nobody), you probably never noticed the deal wasn't what you thought.

I think this is the cleanest way to state the problem we keep circling: not software, but control. The device is a physical object, and we've built several centuries of law around what it means to own one. The manufacturers figured out that if you tie the physical object's function to a remote service you control, you can treat the object as a subscription terminal. The purchase was just you paying for the hardware that gives the subscription somewhere to run, and the ownership was always contingent on conditions written in language you never read. The contingency runs one direction: they decide when the terms change.

Here's the analogy I keep reaching for. For me, it cuts to the bone faster than any abstract legal argument. You buy a restaurant (the building, the equipment, everything). You find out the previous owner retained a contractual right to reach into your kitchen and disable the ovens whenever they liked – or turn them back on only if you kept paying a monthly fee. You'd call that fraud. Call your lawyer. You'd have every expectation the courts would side with you, because we have solid doctrine around what happens when a seller retains covert control over something you've already paid for. We just don't apply that doctrine to devices, because the industry spent two decades persuading the law to treat firmware as a licensed service rather than a component of a product you purchased.

The "right to repair" framing gets at part of this, but I don't think it's the whole story. Repair is about what you can fix; this is about what you can keep. A manufacturer that disables features post-sale isn't preventing you from fixing something broken – they're breaking something functional, deliberately, and then selling you the fix. That's bullshit, and we should stop dressing it up as a service model.

What would it look like to actually fix this? You'd need a rule that says: when you sell a device, the features present at sale are locked in. The manufacturer can push security patches (with your consent). They can offer new features as optional add-ons. They cannot remove existing features, throttle performance, or disable functionality to push you toward a paid tier. The device you bought has to keep doing what it did when you bought it, for as long as the hardware allows. If a feature shipped with the device, that feature belongs to you.

Not a radical ask – kind of boring in ambition, actually. Just ownership: the thing we already said we cared about when we built property law. The manufacturers have spent a decade engineering around that apparatus, one firmware update at a time. We can engineer back. We just have to decide that "bought it" means something, and write that into law before every device we own becomes a subscription we can't cancel.
