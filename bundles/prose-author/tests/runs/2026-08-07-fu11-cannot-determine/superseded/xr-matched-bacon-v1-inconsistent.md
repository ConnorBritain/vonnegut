Small habits are the true architects of a life; intentions are but the scaffolding, which is taken down when the building is done, and no man afterward remembers where it stood. For a man may intend temperance every morning and be intemperate every evening, and the evenings are what he has lived; whereas he that has fallen into the custom of rising early has not intended anything at all, and yet his mornings are his own, and the sum of them is a considerable estate. Certainly the reckoning of a life is made not of what was purposed but of what was repeated.

The reason is plain enough. An intention is a single act of the will, and must be renewed each time it is wanted; a habit is a thing already done, that goes forward of itself and asks nothing. Great resolutions are like a fire of straw, that burns high and is cold within the hour; small usages are like a bank of coals, that gives little light and much heat, and lasts the night. Therefore let a man mistrust the resolution that costs him a great effort to make, and trust rather the practice that costs him none to keep.

It is objected that small things are small, and that a life is judged by its greater passages. But the greater passages are few, and a man comes to them furnished only with what he has practised. He that has been exact in little payments will be found honest in a great one, not because he then resolves upon honesty, but because he has no other motion in him; and he that has been slack in small speech will be found false in a great matter, though he never intended falsehood in his life. The habit does not wait to be consulted. It answers before the man has arrived.

Nor is it otherwise with the mind than with the body. The soldier drilled in the same motion a thousand times does not choose it in the field; he could not choose otherwise if he would. So it is with civility, with attention, with the keeping of an appointed hour: what has been often done is done thereafter without cost, and what is done without cost is what a man will do when he is tired, or afraid, or in haste — and these are the hours in which character is chiefly displayed.

There is a further point, less regarded. Habits are cheap to acquire and dear to be rid of, and men take them on carelessly, as a man takes on a small debt, thinking he may discharge it at his pleasure. But the interest runs. It is easier to plant a hedge than to grub one up, and easier still to let a hedge plant itself; and many a man's disposition is nothing but the hedgerow that grew while he was attending to his intentions.

Let him then govern the small usages and let the great purposes govern themselves, for the first are within his reach daily and the second are not. Let him take the practice he would have and set it where it must be met — at the hour of rising, at the beginning of the meal, at the shutting of the door — so that it is not chosen but encountered. Small stones, laid daily, made the causeway that the army marched over.
