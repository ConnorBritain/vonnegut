Men resolve greatly and live meanly. The resolution is made once, in a good hour, and is remembered with pleasure; the habit is made a hundred times, in indifferent hours, and is not remembered at all; and yet it is the habit that stands when the man is looked at from the outside, for a life is not what a man meant by it, but what he did in it over and over.

Intentions are the cheapest of possessions, for a man may hold a great many at once, and hold them without cost, and be praised for holding them; but habits are dear, and he can carry only so many, and each one has been paid for in the small coin of repetition. Therefore a man is rich in intentions in proportion as he is poor in practice, and the loudest declarer of what he will become is commonly the one who has done least about it.

A habit is like a path worn across a field, which no man set out to make, but which is made nonetheless by the shortest way being taken daily; and when at last a wall is put up, the walkers do not go round, they climb. Therefore, look not to what a man swears he will do when the great occasion comes, but to the way he goes when nothing is at stake.

The small act is small only in its occasion, never in its number. He that is exact in a trifle is not exercising the trifle, but exercising exactness; and he that lets a trifle go is not sparing himself a small labour, but teaching himself that labours may be let go. So the man who answers a letter within the day is not merely punctual in letters. He is building the readiness with which he will one day answer something that matters.

Certainly, the great decisions of a life are decided long before they are put; for when the hour arrives the man does not choose his character, he only spends it, and he will find he has less of it than he supposed, or more, and either way the accounting was done in private and by degrees.

Some hold that a man may keep the habit small and the soul large — that he may be careless in the ordering of his day and yet grand in his affections. But that happeneth rarely. The soul has no other instrument than the day; and a man who has trained no hands cannot execute what his heart designs. As it was with those who wept at the tragedy in the evening and beat their servants in the morning.

Advice, then, is short. Let a man choose one thing, and let it be a thing so small that failure in it is no excuse to him; and let him do it whether he wishes to or not, and particularly when he does not. Not because that one thing will remake him, for it will not, but because the doing of it against inclination is the whole art, and the art is transferable, though the act is not.

And let him beware of the habit he did not choose, which is forming in him at this moment, unopposed, in whatever hour he calls his own — for a man may pull down a house he built, but he sleeps very soundly in one he never noticed being built around him.
