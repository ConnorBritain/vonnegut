```markdown
Ownership is a bundle of rights, and manufacturers have spent the last decade quietly cutting threads out of it.

Think about what it means to own something. You pay for it, you take it home, it sits on your shelf or in your pocket, and you can do what you like with it. That's the deal. But your tractor, your phone, your car, your insulin pump now come with a silent partner you never agreed to: the manufacturer, who retains the ability to reach back through the network and change what the device does. Not repair it. Not improve it. Change what you can do with a thing you bought and paid for.

John Deere has been the canonical example for years (and rightly so, because it's almost too perfect). You buy a $500,000 combine harvester and you can't fix it yourself because the diagnostic software is locked. A sensor fails at harvest, the nearest dealer is three hundred miles away, and the machine sits. You own the steel and the rubber and the engine block. You do not own the software that makes it run. And if John Deere decides tomorrow that a feature your workflow depends on will now require a subscription, there's not a damn thing you can do about it.

But this isn't just about farmers. Think about what happened when Sony updated the PS3 firmware and removed the OtherOS feature from machines people had already purchased. Or when Apple's "Error 53" bricked iPhones that had been repaired by anyone other than Apple. Or when Sonos flipped a software toggle and rendered older speakers into expensive paperweights. In each case, the manufacturer reached into property you'd paid for and changed it. The deed was in your name; the keys were in theirs.

I think people get confused here because we're used to distinguishing between owning a physical object and licensing software. Fair enough as a conceptual matter. But that distinction has become a trap. Manufacturers have used it to reclassify entire devices as software-delivery platforms (the phone isn't the product, it's the access point), which means you never really bought anything. You leased a portal to their ongoing decisions about what that portal should do.

Here's a better frame. Imagine you bought a house and the real-estate company retained the right to enter at any time and remove a room. They could brick up the kitchen. They could take the stairs. After all, the plumbing runs through their proprietary fitting system, and in the terms of service you agreed that they retained management rights over proprietary infrastructure. Would we say you own that house? We'd say you'd been swindled. And we'd make it illegal.

That's where we need to get with connected devices. The right-to-repair movement has done heroic work, and state legislatures are starting to move. But repair is the floor, not the ceiling. The ceiling is a legal regime that says if you sell someone a device, you cannot later reach into it and strip functionality they paid for. Full stop. You can offer updates. You can offer new features. You can offer subscription tiers for genuinely new things. What you cannot do is unilaterally degrade or delete what the buyer reasonably expected to keep.

The manufacturers will tell you this is about security, about keeping devices patched and safe. I don't think it's the whole story. Security updates don't explain why removing a feature means you have to pay again. Safety patches don't explain why your right to fix your own property should require permission from the company that sold it to you.

We've let manufacturers define ownership down to mean "you hold the hardware while we hold the choices." That is not ownership. It's a rental agreement dressed up in a receipt, and we should start treating it like one.
```
