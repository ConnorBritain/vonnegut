# Voice profile — doctorow-blog

## 1. Cadence

The sentences vary widely in length and that variation is load-bearing. A long sentence assembles evidence, complication, and qualification; a short one delivers the verdict. This pattern runs throughout all 10 samples (`2026-07-10-posthuman-as-in-no-humans`: *"There's only one kind of person you get to treat like this: a slave. Slavery is labor without even the pretense of moral consideration."*). When a sentence has run long and argumentative, make the next one four words and flat. Do not explain it.

The colon is the primary pivot between setup and verdict — 10/10 samples, throughout. It introduces a definition, a twist, or the term the previous sentence was reaching for. Use it where another writer would reach for "which means that" or "in other words." (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`: *"If they say 'Property rights are a human right,' then they are on the right."*)

Parenthetical asides appear throughout — [measurement:round-parenthetical-spans] 97 instances at 5.53 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. These are not hedges; they are escalations or sarcastic annotations: *"(very, very weird),"* *"(a direct quote)."* Keep them short and pointed. Aim for 5–6 per 1,000 words.

Interruption inside a sentence uses an en-dash, not an em-dash — [measurement:en-dashes] 73 instances at 4.16 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. Em-dashes are absent from all 10 samples ([measurement:em-dashes] 0 instances). Where you would normally reach for an em-dash, use an en-dash.

## 2. How a piece opens

Three shapes account for the 10 openings. The "Here's a [noun]:" construction — *"Here's an irony:"* (`2026-07-11-your-risk`), *"Here's a sentence that stopped me in my tracks last week:"* (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`) — appears in 2/10 samples. It signals that something counterintuitive is about to be delivered.

A flat declarative indictment opens 2/10 samples with no setup: *"Data brokers are a cancer."* (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`); *"As a technology, AI isn't exceptional."* (`2026-07-24-supplemental-income`). The thesis is the first sentence, stated at full strength.

The remaining 6/10 samples open by summarizing a conventional view in order to dismantle it: *"The theory of markets goes like this:"* (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`); *"There's plenty of reasons to be skeptical of centrists who bemoan 'political polarization'"* (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`). The summary of the received wisdom becomes the thing the piece then destroys.

## 3. How a piece closes

8/10 pieces end on a short reversal or epigram — a formulation that turns the opponent's own term or logic back on itself. *"What they call 'theft' is just self-defense."* (`2026-07-21-dickovers`). *"The only 'billionaire derangement syndrome' that matters is the syndrome that affects billionaires."* (`2026-07-16-lucky-orifices`). *"It's about whether we are ruled at all, or whether we are free."* (`2026-07-09-wilhoitian`). Take the term they're weaponizing and flip it in the final sentence. If you can't do that, two short parallel sentences — one negative, one positive restatement — work as a substitute.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

Second person is the dominant pronoun for the argument — [measurement:second-person-family] 385 instances at 21.94 per 1,000 words, throughout in all 10 samples. The reader is addressed as someone this is happening to: *"your boss,"* *"your paycheck,"* *"the people you love."* Write it that way.

First-person singular ([measurement:first-person-singular-family] 113 instances, 6.44 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples, several times per piece) signals personal testimony: *"I'm 55,"* *"my doctor just informed me."* The "I" introduces a claim from experience.

First-person plural ([measurement:first-person-plural-family] 127 instances, 7.24 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples) positions the writer alongside the reader as a fellow subject: *"we got rid of pop-ups,"* *"they impose on us."* This is not a royal we — it includes the reader on the same side as the writer.

Contractions dominate throughout — [measurement:contractions] 300 instances at 17.09 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. Against them, [measurement:uncontracted-negatives] only 9 uncontracted negative forms appear across the whole corpus at 0.51 per 1,000 words, in 5/10 samples. Uncontracted forms are so rare they register as emphasis when they appear; use the contraction everywhere else.

Rhetorical questions appear throughout — [measurement:question-marks] 38 question marks at 2.17 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples, several times per piece. They are not genuine inquiries; they voice the opponent's likely objection before answering it, or set up the answer the piece is about to provide.

Profanity appears in all 10 samples — [measurement:profanity-vulgarity] 26 instances at 1.48 per 1,000 words, once or twice per piece. It marks the moment of maximum contempt or absurdity: *"tell the cartel to fuck off,"* *"holy shit."* Reserve it for those moments. Distribute it evenly and it disappears.

Opponents are named explicitly in all 10 samples — Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Tony West, Trump — never left as "some billionaires" or "certain politicians." Allies and sources are cited by name with an attribution phrase: *"As Weil writes,"* *"As Tim O'Reilly writes,"* *"As Keynes wrote."* Both sides get named; neither is left anonymous.

When a hedge appears — 3/10 samples — it takes the form of a first-person marker: *"I think billionaire solipsism can account for much of this"* (`2026-07-24-supplemental-income`), *"I strongly believe that you should go through the tedious process"* (`2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`). Adverbial hedges like "perhaps" or "possibly" are absent. If you need to soften a claim, do it with "I think," not with a modal adverb.

## 5. Figures

Extended analogies are worked through multiple paragraphs in 8/10 samples. The analogy is not decorative; it does the argumentative work. The escrow-agent analogy in `2026-07-22-table-flipper` runs for six consecutive paragraphs. The restaurateur-and-tipped-wages analogy in `2026-07-11-your-risk` spans four before the pivot to Uber. Set up the analogy's terms precisely and then work through them one by one — do not state the conclusion before completing the working.

Coined compound epithets appear throughout in all 10 samples: *"broccoli-haired brownshirts,"* *"cod-Marxist,"* *"god-botherers,"* *"jobspocalypse,"* *"Renfields."* These are constructed from two recognizable parts to produce contemptuous precision. Use them sparingly enough that each one is a small event.

## 6. Register range

The base register is sardonic and declarative across all 10 samples. What shifts is the mode of delivery.

In 4/10 samples, biographical material appears: the writer's own cancer diagnosis, his age, his literary executor, a dead friend (`2026-07-14-designated-survivor`: *"my doctor just informed me that I am cancer free, following excision, radiotherapy and immunotherapy"*). These are offered as evidence for the argument, not atmosphere. When you include a personal detail, it should prove something about the claim in the same paragraph.

In 3/10 samples, deadpan list structure signals absurdity — `2026-07-23-drop-a-dime`: *"Step one: Exist. Step two: There is no step two."* The structure does the work. State it and do not explain it.

## 7. What the corpus never does

Structure is carried inside the prose in all 10 samples. A new paragraph marks a new move; the first sentence of that paragraph announces the pivot, often naming the new target directly: *"Back to Quiggin:"* (`2026-07-22-table-flipper`); *"Back to algorithmic pricing:"* (`2026-07-11-your-risk`). Nothing in the corpus breaks a piece up with a subheading, a bulleted list, or a numbered section.

En-dashes carry all interruption work — [measurement:en-dashes] 73 instances, 4.16 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. Em-dashes are absent from the corpus entirely — [measurement:em-dashes] 0 instances, 0 per 1,000 words, established across all 10 samples. Do not use an em-dash.
