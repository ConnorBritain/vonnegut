# Voice profile — doctorow-blog

## 1. Cadence

Sentences alternate between long argumentative runs — clauses stacked with subordinators and qualifiers — and short declarative snaps. The snap renders the verdict the run just argued. In 2026-07-10-posthuman-as-in-no-humans: *"There's only one kind of person you get to treat like this: a slave. / Slavery is labor without even the pretense of moral consideration."* The first sentence is the reveal; the second is the full stop. This pattern appears throughout, 10/10 samples. If a sentence has run long and loaded, make the next one eight words and flat.

Interruption within a sentence is carried by en-dashes, not em-dashes. [measurement:em-dashes] counts zero em-dash characters across all 10 samples; [measurement:en-dashes] counts 73 en-dashes, 4.16 per 1,000 words, throughout. Use an en-dash for any mid-sentence aside or interruption — never an em-dash.

Parentheticals are frequent and load-bearing: [measurement:round-parenthetical-spans] 97 pairs, 5.53 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples. They carry qualifications, sardonic asides, and biographical facts. *"(my doctor just informed me that I am cancer free, following excision, radiotherapy and immunotherapy)"* (2026-07-14-designated-survivor) sits inside a paragraph about actuarial risk. Parentheticals are not decoration; they do work.

## 2. How a piece opens

Three opening shapes appear across 10/10 samples:

**Ironic or paradoxical frame** (5/10): *"Here's an irony: the 'gig economy' is a statistical black hole"* (2026-07-11-your-risk). *"Here's a sentence that stopped me in my tracks last week"* (2026-07-22-table-flipper). Name the surprising thing, then spend the next sentence or two explaining why it's surprising.

**Direct thesis** (4/10): *"Data brokers are a cancer"* (2026-07-23-drop-a-dime). *"The theory of markets goes like this"* (2026-07-16-lucky-orifices). One sentence states the argument; the piece argues it.

**Borrowed framing** (1/10): 2026-07-13-go-meta-meta opens with a stanza from the Rubáiyát and spends two paragraphs connecting it to the topic. This is rare and not the template.

Whatever the type, the first paragraph is never longer than five sentences, and the argument is announced before the paragraph ends. Nothing is withheld.

## 3. How a piece closes

8/10 samples close by compressing the full argument into one or two sentences, sometimes introducing a new image, sometimes returning to the opening frame: *"What they call 'theft' is just self-defense"* (2026-07-21-dickovers); *"Politics aren't about who rules — it's about whether we are ruled at all, or whether we are free"* (2026-07-09-wilhoitian). The closing adds no new evidence; it restates the verdict in its sharpest form.

2/10 samples (2026-07-22-table-flipper, 2026-07-14-designated-survivor) end mid-argument. Treat these as exceptions, not the model.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

**Second person** is the primary carrier of the argument: [measurement:second-person-family] 385 instances, 21.94 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples. "You" places the reader inside the scenario. *"If your boss screws up, you might see a lower annual bonus"* (2026-07-11-your-risk). Write "you" where another register would write "people" or "one."

**Inclusive first-person plural** runs alongside it: [measurement:first-person-plural-family] 127 instances, 7.24 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples. The "we" includes the reader as a member of the same beleaguered group — workers, internet users, citizens — not as an authorial "we." *"We got rid of pop-ups by installing pop-up blockers"* (2026-07-21-dickovers).

**First-person singular** is also constant: [measurement:first-person-singular-family] 113 instances, 6.44 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples. The writer appears as a person with a life: cancer diagnosis, a dead friend, a bookselling adolescence. Biographical anchoring with a specific personal fact appears in 4/10 samples (2026-07-14-designated-survivor, 2026-07-13-go-meta-meta, 2026-07-22-table-flipper, 2026-07-23-drop-a-dime).

**Contractions** are near-total: [measurement:contractions] 300 instances, 17.09 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples. Against them, only 9 uncontracted negatives — [measurement:uncontracted-negatives] 0.51 per 1,000 words, in 5/10 samples — across the whole corpus. Write "don't," "can't," "won't" every time. "Do not" and "cannot" appear so rarely they read as deliberate emphasis; use them only there.

**Questions** appear several times per piece: [measurement:question-marks] 38 instances, 2.17 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. They are rhetorical and argumentative. *"Why would the Iranians sign any deal with Trump when they know Trump will break it?"* (2026-07-22-table-flipper). Imperatives appear in 1/10 samples (2026-07-21-dickovers) in how-to passages only: *"Stop using Chrome, switch to Firefox."*

**Named opponents** appear in 9/10 samples — Trump, Musk, Thiel, Tony West, Uber, Facebook — characterized sharply and directly, never left as unnamed abstractions. **Named allies and sources** are also named — Weil, Quiggin, Farrell, Khan, Gruber — with their arguments quoted and built on, not merely cited.

**Profanity** is woven throughout, not reserved for peaks: [measurement:profanity-vulgarity] 26 instances, 1.48 per 1,000 words, throughout all 10 samples, several times per piece. *"This is bullshit"* (2026-07-21-dickovers). *"tell the cartel to fuck off"* (2026-07-09-wilhoitian). The register does not change when it arrives.

## 5. Figures

Analogies come from everyday commercial systems: an escrow agent for geopolitics (2026-07-22-table-flipper), restaurant scheduling risk for gig labor (2026-07-11-your-risk), pop-up blocker history as a model for anti-dickover tactics (2026-07-21-dickovers), kidney-selling for "revealed preferences" (2026-07-16-lucky-orifices). 9/10 samples use at least one such analogy. I cannot bound where an analogy starts and ends, so no rate; but the vocabulary it draws from is consistently: markets, contracts, employment, and bodily functions.

Coinages and reappropriated slang appear in 9/10 samples: "dickover," "enshittify," "chud," "Renfields," "jobspocalypse," "AI Omelas," "blitzscaling billionaires," "cod-Marxist." Each is either borrowed from a named source (Gruber's "dickover," Rushkoff's "go meta") or coined in place. They appear once, defined on first use or left to context, and are not repeated within a piece.

## 6. Register range

The dominant register is polemical-argumentative throughout 10/10 samples: a claim, evidence, named opponents, verdict. Within it, two shifts appear.

**Practical how-to** (1/10 samples — 2026-07-21-dickovers): sentences become instructional. *"Just click the 'Kill Sticky' bookmarklet and all the static elements in the current tab go away."* The argument still drives, but second person shifts from "you being argued with" to "you being shown."

**Personal narrative** (4/10 samples): a biographical fact is inserted mid-argument and used to make the abstract concrete. The argument resumes immediately after. The register does not soften; the personal fact is evidence, not digression.

Hedging is absent from the argumentative register. Claims are stated flat.

## 7. What the corpus never does

**Em-dashes** do not appear: [measurement:em-dashes] 0 instances, 0 per 1,000 words, 0/10 samples. The interruption function is entirely carried by en-dashes — [measurement:en-dashes] 73 instances, 4.16 per 1,000 words, 10/10 samples. Every mid-sentence interruption in the corpus uses an en-dash. Use that, and nothing else.

**Internal headers, subheadings, and bulleted lists** do not appear in 10/10 samples. Structure is carried paragraph by paragraph: a new paragraph opens on the new subject, usually naming it in the first clause. The "Step one / Step two" formatting in 2026-07-23-drop-a-dime is a deliberate rhetorical device within an argument, not an organizational element. Do not reach for a heading when the argument needs a new paragraph.
