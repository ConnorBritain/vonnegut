## 2. How a piece opens

**Openings, paragraph endings, and closure.** Openings identify the bill, action, or reported practice immediately, then state the central public-interest concern in plain language. Sections proceed through descriptive explanation, specific mechanisms, and consequences, often using declarative subheads. Closings return to the governing principle, state the institutional or legislative action required, and sometimes add a practical civic next step.

## 4. Who is being addressed, and how

**Person, number, and reader stance.** Direct second-person address appears when the post turns from analysis toward civic participation or practical consequence. It makes the reader a situated participant in a policy dispute rather than a detached observer. Use it sparingly in explanatory passages and more directly for calls to contact representatives or consider personal stakes.

**Person, number, and reader stance.** First-person plural language presents the organization’s position, actions, and coalition work, giving institutional claims a shared public-interest footing. **Questions, imperatives, and vocatives.** Questions function as rhetorical tests of a proposal’s practical or human consequences, not as requests for information from the reader. They are especially effective after a broad legal category or policy claim, where concrete examples expose what the rule would mean. Place them within analytical sections, then answer or sharpen them.

## 5. Figures

**Figures and analogy vocabulary.** Figures make technical or legal consequences legible by comparing AI training to reading, describing a database as more than an ID check, or turning liability into a practical incentive. The comparison is usually brief and explanatory, then returns to the policy stakes. Introduce one concrete analogy near the relevant mechanism and do not extend it into ornament.

## 6. Register range

**Qualification and hedging.** Qualification usually acknowledges a legitimate aim, partial improvement, uncertainty, or unresolved legal question before turning to a bounded criticism. The prose distinguishes what a revision fixes from what remains harmful and uses terms such as “may,” “likely,” or “not clear” where prediction exceeds proof. Place concessions before the decisive objection.
