Here’s the puzzle: when you buy a device, what exactly have you bought? You’ve paid for the object in your hands, but the argument here is that ownership should include control over whether its maker can disable features after sale. That sounds obvious until you notice the little trapdoor beneath the word “own.”

If a maker can reach into a device after the sale and switch off something you bought, then your ownership is conditional (and the condition is controlled by someone else). You can hold the device, carry it around, customize it, and depend on it, while the maker retains a remote hand on the controls. That’s less like owning a tool than renting a room whose landlord can rearrange the furniture whenever it suits them.

Of course, makers need room to fix defects, address security problems, and change services that genuinely can’t remain frozen forever. I’m not arguing that every feature must last unchanged for eternity. I’m arguing for a boundary: fixing a problem is different from withdrawing a capability, and maintaining a device is different from quietly reducing what it can do. Those distinctions matter because the buyer has already made the central bargain.

What happens when the feature you rely on becomes a permission the maker can revoke? You’re left with the physical shell of your purchase and a software-shaped “maybe” hovering over it. The device still looks like yours, but its useful boundaries have been redrawn somewhere you can’t see. That’s the bullshit at the center of control without ownership.

We should be especially suspicious of the phrase “after sale.” The sale is the moment when money, expectation, and responsibility are supposed to line up. You pay, the maker provides the thing, and we understand what each side is allowed to do next. If the maker can later disable a feature, then the transaction hasn’t ended in the ordinary sense; it has become an ongoing negotiation in which you’ve already paid and the other side still holds the veto.

I think the technical question is simpler than the industry-shaped fog around it. Can the device continue doing the thing you bought it to do without asking permission from its maker? If the answer depends on a remote decision, an account status, or a change you can’t inspect, then the problem isn’t merely inconvenience. It’s that the device’s behavior has been separated from your control.

You might say that the maker created the feature, so the maker should be free to remove it. That confuses authorship with authority. A maker can design a capability, document it, and support it, while the buyer still receives a reasonable claim to keep using it (subject to clear, narrow limits). We don’t treat every purchased object as a temporary invitation to the seller’s future preferences. A maker’s continuing involvement can be valuable, but it shouldn’t turn the buyer into a guest in their own device.

I want to stress the practical consequence for you. When you choose a device, you’re also choosing a relationship with the maker’s decisions. That relationship should include updates and repairs, but it shouldn’t include an invisible button marked “make this purchase smaller.” Our devices shouldn’t become evidence lockers for promises that can vanish later.

The rule we need is modest: owning a device should include control over whether its maker can disable features after sale. That rule leaves room for safety fixes, honest end-of-support policies, and features that were plainly temporary from the start. It simply says that a sale should transfer meaningful control, not just possession. You shouldn’t have to keep proving that you own the thing you already bought. If ownership can be switched off, it was never ownership in the first place.
