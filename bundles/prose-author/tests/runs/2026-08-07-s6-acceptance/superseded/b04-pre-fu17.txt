The pitch has been the same for as long as there's been an industry to make it, and it goes like this. Advertising is a tax you pay in attention, and the only thing that makes the tax bearable is relevance. An ad for the thing you were going to buy anyway isn't an intrusion, it's a service. To be relevant, an ad has to know something about you; the more it knows, the better it guesses; and the better it guesses, the less it feels like an ad at all. Yes, there was an awkward adolescence — the shoes that trailed you around the web for a month after you'd already bought the shoes, the baby coupons that turned up before you'd told your own family — but that was a growing pain, and growing pains pass. The industry is maturing now. On-device. Privacy-preserving. Cohort-based, aggregated, federated. Any year now, the ads will know exactly what you want and nothing whatsoever about who you are.

That's one dial. They are selling you both ends of it.

Accuracy in this business isn't a property of the ad. It's a property of the file. An ad doesn't get better the way a joke gets better; it gets better the way a bloodhound gets better, by getting closer to something that came off your body. Every increment of relevance you've been promised is an increment of resolution on you — where you slept, what you typed into a search box at 2am and deleted, whose wifi your phone shook hands with, which pharmacy you walked past slowly. There is no setting on this machine that knows you well enough to be useful and vaguely enough to be polite.

Which raises the question the relevance guys never answer in public: if the targeting really works without the surveillance, why are they fighting so fucking hard to keep the surveillance? Because it doesn't. Give them a cohort and they'll de-anonymize it. Give them a hash and they'll join it to four other hashes. Give them a promise and they'll ship the promise and keep the pipe.

The industry's favorite picture of itself is the corner shop. The guy behind the counter knows you take two sugars, knows your kid's name, knows to put the good bread aside on a Friday, and nobody thinks that's sinister. That's just being known by someone you buy things from.

Except the guy behind the counter can't sell what he knows to seventeen brokers. He can't be subpoenaed for it. He can't be breached at three in the morning by somebody in another country who now has your Fridays. And crucially, he found out you take two sugars because you stood in front of him and told him, which is the entire difference between a relationship and a stranger going through your bins and calling it a conversation.

So when the pixel-stuffers reach for the corner shop, notice which half of it they're borrowing. They want the warmth of being known. They do not want the part where you could stop telling him.

Here's the tell, and I think it's the only one you need. When Apple put a box on the screen that asked people in plain language whether they wanted to be tracked across other companies' apps, the overwhelming majority said no, and an entire sector treated this as a natural disaster rather than a result. Nobody said: fine, we'll target on context, we'll sell against the article. They said: our accuracy has been destroyed. That's the argument, made under oath, in their own words, on their own behalf.

Ethan Zuckerman called advertising the internet's original sin, and Shoshana Zuboff gave the mature form of it a name — surveillance capitalism — and both of those are more polite than what's actually being run here, which is closer to George Akerlof's market for lemons. You can't inspect the file. You can't price the harm. You can't tell the honest broker from the crooked one, because they buy from the same (very, very grubby) intermediaries and swear the same oath about anonymization. They call it relevance when they're selling to advertisers, personalization when they're selling to you, and signal when they're selling to each other.

The next standard is already in the post, and it will arrive wearing a name with "privacy" in it. It's the same dial, and the hand on it is not yours. They keep promising to take the creepy out of the accuracy. There is no creepy to take out: creepy is their word for the accuracy working.
