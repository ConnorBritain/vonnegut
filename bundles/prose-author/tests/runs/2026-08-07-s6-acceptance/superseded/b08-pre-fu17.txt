Your bank does not charge you an overdraft fee because you did something wrong. Your bank charges you an overdraft fee because it is a good business, and it is a good business in the way that a slot machine is a good business: the house does not need every player to lose, it needs a reliable minority to lose over and over again forever.

Here is the part that gets left out of the personal-finance advice column. Overdraft revenue is not spread thinly across everybody who occasionally miscounts. It concentrates. A small slice of account-holders — people running balances near zero, which is to say people who are poor — generate the overwhelming bulk of it. Everyone else in the system is essentially a bystander, enjoying free checking that is not free at all, but is instead paid for by the guy whose rent cleared on Thursday and whose gas station charge cleared on Wednesday. He's underwriting your no-fee account. Nobody told him he'd been enrolled.

Think about what that does to the incentive. If overdraft fees were really a penalty — a deterrent, a nudge, a stern look from a man in a waistcoat — then the bank would want fewer of them. A deterrent that never deters is a failed product, and a failed product gets killed. But overdraft isn't a deterrent, it's a revenue line, and revenue lines get optimized. When the thing you're measuring is the number of times your customer's account goes underwater, and your quarterly numbers go up when that number goes up, you do not build a system that stops it from happening. You build a system that helps it along and then apologizes.

And they did build it. The classic version is transaction reordering: you make four purchases in a day, three small and one large, and the bank posts the largest one first so that the three little ones each bounce instead of one. Same money, same day, same account, four times the fees. That's not a bug in a batch-processing system designed in 1974, that's a choice about the order of a list. Somebody wrote that sort. Somebody reviewed the pull request.

The tell is what happens whenever anyone tries to make it stop. Banks don't defend overdraft by defending the sort order — they defend it by saying that customers value the service, that people want their transactions to go through, that a declined card at the pharmacy counter is worse than a fee. Which would be a great argument if the alternative to a $35 fee were a declined card, rather than the other thing a bank could obviously do, which is decline the card for free. That option exists. It costs nothing. It is not on the menu.

I think this is why the fight over overdraft never quite reads as a fight about consumer protection, even though that's the aisle it's shelved in. It's a fight about whether a bank is allowed to sell you a very small, very expensive, involuntary loan at a moment when you have no ability to say no, and then call the price of that loan a penalty so that nobody has to describe the interest rate out loud. Because you can do that math. Thirty-five dollars on a twelve-dollar shortfall, paid back in three days, is an annualized rate that would make a payday lender blush and a loan shark ask for a referral.

The banks know exactly what they've got. That's why the fee survives every round of outrage, every apologetic press release, every "we've listened to our customers" refresh of the terms of service in which the fee is trimmed by five dollars and the daily cap is raised by one. The machine is not malfunctioning. The machine is running.

So the next time your bank tells you overdraft protection is a courtesy, take the word seriously and follow it where it goes. A courtesy is something done for you at the doer's expense. Thirty-five dollars is not a courtesy. It's a cover charge for being broke, collected at the door, from the only people who can't afford to leave.
