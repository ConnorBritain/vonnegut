A digital purchase is a rental that has been taught to lie about its name.

You clicked a button that said Buy, on a page that said Your Library, at a price that was not a rental price, and for four years the show sat in the grid with everything else you'd bought, behaving exactly the way a shelf behaves. Then a contract between two companies you have never heard of, covering territories you have never been to, ran out on some Tuesday nobody mentioned. Now there's a hole in the grid and a receipt in your inbox that still says Purchased.

So ask the question the terms of service already answered in a font you'd need a magnifying glass for: if you bought it, how can it lapse? It lapses because you didn't buy it. You bought a revocable licence to request a stream of a file that lives on someone else's computer, good for exactly as long as that someone keeps paying a third someone, and the word on the button was Buy because "Rent, Indefinite Term, Landlord May Evict Without Notice" doesn't move units. The button is the whole fucking con.

Buy a chest freezer. Not financed, not leased — bought, delivered, wedged into the garage between the paint cans and the bikes, packed with a summer of blueberries and most of a cow.

Now a truck pulls up, because the freezer company's arrangement with the compressor company has lapsed, and two guys with a dolly take the freezer, and the blueberries go grey in the driveway, and an email explains that the freezer is no longer available in your region. You would not call this a licensing matter. You'd call the cops, and the cops would come, because taking a thing out of a person's garage is a crime with a name and a sentence attached to it.

The only reason nobody comes when the third season vanishes is that it was never in your garage. It was in theirs the whole time. So who's out anything here? You are, and only you: the show still exists, the studio still has it, the store still has the shelf space, and your twenty dollars is still spent. "Your Library" is a web page that describes their garage.

Ask the rights-shufflers where it went and they'll tell you the deal expired. Ask them why they sold you a thing whose existence depended on that deal and they'll tell you the deal expired. Ask for the twenty dollars and they'll tell you the deal expired. It's a phrase built to make a decision sound like weather — a front moved in, the show blew away, nobody's hands were anywhere near it. Bullshit. Somebody at a desk ran a job that deleted a row with your name on it.

You could fix this yourself in an afternoon: break the DRM, keep the file, own the thing you paid for the way you owned a DVD, which is to say boringly and forever. Section 1201 of the DMCA makes that a federal crime — not the copying, the lock-breaking on its own. So why is the lock still there after the licence evaporates? Because the lock was never about the licence. It's what stops the customer from outlasting the contract.

I think the tell is what happens to the money, which is nothing. When California passed AB 2426 and made it illegal to put Buy on a digital good you can revoke without saying so, the answer from the clearance clerks wasn't refunds, or escrow, or a downloadable copy — it was a (very, very lightly) reworded button and a disclosure nobody reads. They will change any word you make them change. They will not change the arrangement the word describes.

In 2023, Sony told PlayStation Store customers that Discovery shows they had bought were coming out of their libraries. So what did your money actually buy? A seat in a negotiation between licence-jugglers you'll never meet, each of them assuming the other one is holding the bag, and the bag is you. You are not a customer here. You're a fucking tenant.

A library — and they picked that word, not me — is the one building in town whose entire job is that the thing is still there when you come back for it. Rain, budget cuts, a change of management: still there. They built the precise opposite of that and called it a library anyway, because the word does work the product can't. The licence that lapsed wasn't the studio's. It was yours.
