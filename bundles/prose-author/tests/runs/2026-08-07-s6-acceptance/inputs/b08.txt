An overdraft fee is a product. Not a penalty, not a fine, not a disappointed little tut from an institution that wishes you'd been more careful with your money — a product, with a revenue line, a quarterly target, and a vice-president somewhere whose bonus depends on you being sixty dollars short on a Tuesday. Penalties are meant to stop happening. Products are meant to ship.

You have thirty-two dollars in your account on Thursday morning. You buy a four-dollar coffee, you put nine dollars of gas in the car, you spend six on a sandwich, and at six in the evening you buy forty dollars of groceries because the fridge has become a cave with a lightbulb in it. Four purchases, one balance, and only the last one is bigger than what's in there.

Run those in the order you made them and three clear, one bounces, and you owe one fee. So your bank does not run them in the order you made them. The reorder artists put the groceries through first, while there's still money to almost cover them, and then bounce the coffee and the gas and the sandwich in behind it, each one its own separate little catastrophe, each one thirty-five dollars. Same money. Same day. Same you. One hundred and forty dollars instead of thirty-five.

Ask the sequencing priesthood why the order matters and you'll be told it's so your most important payments clear first — the rent, the mortgage, the car note, the ones where bouncing really hurts. Fine. Now answer the next question, which the brochure cannot: if the point is protecting the big payment, why does the fee attach to the small ones? Why does saving your rent cost you four fees instead of one? It isn't sequencing. It's a harvest, and they're the ones holding the fucking scythe.

The harvest comes off a very small field. Most people with a checking account overdraw approximately never, and the money comes instead from a thin, stable, exhaustively mapped slice of accountholders who overdraw again and again, because they are running a household on a balance that hovers within one bad Thursday of zero. That is not a bug somebody hasn't gotten around to fixing. That's the goddamned business model. The (extremely load-bearing) customer segment here is people who are broke, and the industry has known precisely who they are, by name and by ZIP code, for a very long time.

Which is why nothing the overdraft dukes do makes sense as deterrence and all of it makes sense as merchandising. If a fee were a warning, would you design the account so the charge goes through rather than declining it for free? Would you bury the opt-in inside an onboarding flow and put the opt-out at the end of a phone tree? Would you name the thing that empties your account "protection"? You would not. You'd decline the card at the register, your customer would be embarrassed for four seconds, and nobody would make a fucking dime. The embarrassment is free. The dime is the point.

I think the reason this one makes people angrier than any other line on a statement is that it is so nakedly a machine for charging you money for the crime of not having any money. A parking ticket is at least about the parking. This is a vending machine that surcharges you for not having exact change, installed by the people who count your change: the closer you get to zero, the more it costs you to be there, and the more it costs you to be there, the closer you get to zero. The trap is baited with your own paycheck. It doesn't need anybody to spring it, and that is the part the design gets right.

So the courtesy-pay clergy will tell you this is a service. They call it courtesy, they call it coverage, they call it something you signed up for — and the tell is that no other business on earth charges thirty-five dollars for the courtesy of not saying no to you. A grocer who "protected" you from an empty wallet by ringing up your cart and billing you for the favor would be run out of town by lunchtime. The only thing overdraft protection protects is the overdraft.
