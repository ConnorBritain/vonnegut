Academic publishing is a protection racket. The research was paid for out of your taxes, carried out by people on public salaries in buildings your bond measure financed, written up on evenings and weekends that nobody billed for, and then checked — line by line, method by method, does-the-data-actually-say-what-the-abstract-claims — by other people on public salaries who did it for free, as a professional courtesy, at two in the morning, between grading. Then the whole bundle was handed over to a company that locked it behind a paywall and sold it back to the university that made it.

Fund the research and they'll charge you for it. Review the research and they'll charge you for it. Write the research and they'll charge you eleven thousand dollars for the privilege of letting strangers read your own goddamn sentences.

Here is the part that should make you put down your coffee. If the writing is free, and the reviewing is free, and the editing is mostly done by an academic who gets a line on a CV and a bottle of wine at Christmas, what exactly is the university buying? It is buying a name. That's it. It is renting a font and a masthead, and the going rate for that rental has held profit margins north of thirty percent for years — margins that would embarrass a cartel, sustained by a product whose entire supply chain works for nothing.

Imagine a restaurant that operates this way. You bring the ingredients. You bring them because you grew them, in a field the city bought, using a tractor the city bought. You walk into the kitchen and you cook the meal yourself, and while you're cooking, the guy at the next station — also unpaid, also a farmer, also there because it's what one does — tastes your sauce and tells you it needs acid and that your reduction is broken. You fix it. You plate it. You wash up.

Then the restaurant puts your dinner under a heat lamp behind a locked glass case, and when you ask for it back, they inform you that access to the case is sold as an annual subscription, bundled with four hundred meals you didn't cook and don't want, at a price they will not let you disclose to the farmer down the road. And if you pick the lock, that's a felony.

You would burn that restaurant down. Universities signed a five-year renewal.

The bundling is not incidental, it's the whole mechanism. You cannot buy the twelve journals your chemistry department actually needs; you buy the twelve and the two thousand, priced as one lump, under a nondisclosure agreement that keeps the university across the state from finding out it's paying double for the same thing. These toll-booth people learned the trick from cable television, and cable television learned it from anyone who has ever sold a mandatory extra alongside a thing you had no choice about buying.

And the (very, very effective) reason it holds is that the thing being sold isn't articles. It's tenure. A young researcher does not submit to a journal because the journal will typeset her paper nicely. She submits because a hiring committee will run its finger down her list of venues, and the venues are ranked by a number the publishers themselves helped make load-bearing. Prestige is a currency, and these barnacles own the mint.

Which is why every clean-sounding fix gets absorbed. Open access was supposed to knock the wall down; instead the wall was moved to the other end of the pipe, and now the author pays. Same money, same margins, new invoice, and a graduate student in Lagos or Belgrade who cannot pay it simply does not appear in the record — not rejected, just priced out, which is a quieter kind of censorship and a far more durable one.

I think we misdiagnose this as a pricing problem. It isn't. When Alexandra Elbakyan stood up Sci-Hub, millions of researchers with legitimate institutional access used it anyway, because it was faster than their own libraries — the pirate site was the better product, built by one person, for nothing. When Aaron Swartz downloaded from JSTOR, the response was thirteen felony counts. That tells you what's actually being defended, and it isn't scholarship.

So stop calling it publishing. Nothing is being published; the work was already written, already vetted, already finished before these people touched it. Peer review is the one genuine service in the entire arrangement, and the publishers didn't build it, don't pay for it, and couldn't reconstitute it if it walked out tomorrow. The only review they have ever actually performed is the quarterly one, for shareholders.
