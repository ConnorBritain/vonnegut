The ad-tech industry has exactly one dial.

Every eighteen months the pixel-stuffers roll the same reassurance out in a new font: the tracking is getting less invasive and more effective at the same time, the ads will know what you want without anyone knowing who you are, your data will be hashed and aggregated and anonymized and held on your own phone inside a cryptographic lockbox no human being will ever open — and also, simultaneously, the ad you get will be aimed so precisely at the thing you were thinking about in the shower this morning that you'll flip your phone over to check the mic light. Those aren't two promises. That's one dial with a different label painted at each end, and they're selling both ends in the same slide deck, to the same buyers, with the same straight face.

Turn it down and the ad is a shot in the dark. Turn it up and the ad is a stranger reciting the contents of your medicine cabinet. Turn it all the way up and the ad tells your employer something about you before you've told your spouse. There's no fourth setting, because the accuracy *is* the intrusion: a "relevant" ad is a correct guess about something you never offered, and a correct guess about something you never offered is the whole fucking definition of creepy. Two decades of engineering went into a machine whose only product is that feeling, and the same firms now sell you a second product to make the feeling go away.

Here's the question none of these creeps will take in public: if the targeting really works without knowing anything about you, what is all the data for? It isn't rhetorical, and the answer is boring. The data is for the targeting. Nobody spends fifteen years and a lobbying budget defending the right to keep a file they don't need, and nobody builds a real-time auction around an audience they can't describe.

Think about the guy behind the deli counter who knows your order. That's the good version, the one the (endlessly reshot) industry sizzle reel keeps dramatizing: you walk in, he's already slicing, you're back out in ninety seconds with the sandwich you wanted. Nobody finds that sinister. He learned it by watching you, in one shop, over years, and the knowledge sits in his head and dies there.

Now bolt a scanner over his door. He knows your order, and he knows you stopped buying pastrami in March, and he knows you switched to low-sodium turkey four days after the pharmacy two blocks over sold you something, and he knows which weeks you buy two sandwiches and which weeks you buy one. He hasn't gotten better at sandwiches. He's gotten better at you.

The file is the business, not the sandwich, so the file gets sold: your landlord buys it, your insurer buys it, a background-check outfit buys it and joins it to eleven others, and not one of them has ever wanted lunch.

The deli guy was never the product. The scanner was, which is why every reform that leaves the scanner bolted up there gets absorbed inside a product cycle. Kill the third-party cookie and they build a fingerprint. Kill the fingerprint and they build a login wall. Kill the login wall and they buy your location off the weather app you installed for the pollen count. That isn't a loophole, that's the fucking business, and a business routes around a rule the way water routes around a rock.

None of this is a hidden finding. Shoshana Zuboff named the arrangement "surveillance capitalism" and the industry took it as a compliment. Bruce Schneier put it flatter: surveillance is the business model of the internet. Google spent years promising to end the third-party cookie in Chrome, shipped the Privacy Sandbox and its Topics API as the humane replacement, then reversed the deprecation and left the cookie sitting exactly where it was, running alongside its own successor. If the on-device math really did the job, why keep the cookie? Because the math was never the point. The inventory was, and I think the tell is that the humane replacement always ships after the regulator shows up and never before.

And if the cohort really is anonymous, why is it worth more the smaller it gets? Because anonymity that survives targeting has nothing to sell. The entire (deeply unserious) pitch is a guy sniffing your laundry and then invoicing you for the service.

So when the next privacy ghoul promises you relevance without surveillance, ask him which way he's turning the dial. He can make the ads less creepy. He can make the ads more accurate. He cannot do both, and the reason isn't cryptographic and it isn't regulatory. Creepy is just accurate, described from your side of the counter.
