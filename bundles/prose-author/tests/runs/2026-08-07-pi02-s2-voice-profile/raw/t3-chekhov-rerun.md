# Voice profile — chekhov-correspondence

These are letters in Constance Garnett's English translation, printed as a selection. Everything below about grammar and structure is safe to imitate; everything about punctuation carries a caveat, restated in section 8.

## 1. How the sentences run

The default motion is accumulation by comma and semicolon: a subject is stated, then the evidence for it piles up as a run of noun phrases rather than as new sentences — 10/10 samples (`chekhov-043`: *"Rocks, crags, forests, thousands of ducks, herons and all sorts of beaked gentry, and absolute wilderness."*; `chekhov-154`: *"a big garden, beyond the garden, mountains covered with forest, few people, little movement in the street."*). To do this, resist starting a new sentence for the second item. Let the list keep going on commas until it is genuinely finished.

What ends the accumulation is a short flat sentence, usually under eight words, usually a bare statement or a verdict — 9/10 samples (`chekhov-056`, after four lines of climbing Vesuvius: *"I believe in hell now."*; `chekhov-017`, after the long sentence about migrating birds: *"Life is hard in this world!"*). This is the most reproducible habit in the corpus: if a sentence has run long by piling up, make the next one very short and do not decorate it. The exception is `chekhov-115`, which sustains its long clauses to the end of the letter without ever dropping to a short one.

Paragraphs are uneven on purpose. A paragraph of one or two sentences sits between long ones and is not expanded — 6/10 samples (`chekhov-056`: *"Suvorin _fils_ lost 300 francs."*; `chekhov-043`: *"To-day I changed into the steamer _Muravyov_, which they say does not rock; maybe I shall write."*).

## 2. How a letter opens

Nine of the ten openings are one of two shapes — 9/10 samples. Either a situation report, the writer's place, weather or body stated flatly before anything else (`chekhov-132`: *"The winter is very cold, I am not well, no one has written to me for nearly a whole month"*), or a pickup of the recipient's last letter, entering mid-exchange (`chekhov-115`: *"In your letter there is a text from Scripture."*). The tenth, `chekhov-087`, opens on a *"Yes"* answering a remark that is not reproduced, and belongs cleanly to neither.

Whichever shape, the first sentence names a place or a physical condition inside its first clause — 7/10 samples (`chekhov-001`: *"I got your letter when I was fearfully bored and was sitting at the gate yawning"*). Nothing is set up; the letter starts where the writer is.

## 3. How a letter closes

The last thing said is smaller than the largest thing said — a small request, a domestic fact, an ache — 6/10 samples (`chekhov-031`, ending a letter that has covered the Volga, the Urals and a family quarrel: *"Ask Lika not to leave such big margins in her letters."*; `chekhov-132`: *"Nadyezhda Ivanovna has gone off to Moscow."*). The usable instruction: whatever the letter's biggest thought was, do not end on it. Put a small concrete one after it.

A generalizing one-clause maxim turns up among the last three sentences — 4/10 samples (`chekhov-056`: *"One must draw the line."*; `chekhov-087`: *"The more objective, the stronger will be the effect."*).

## 4. Who is addressed, and how

Always one named person, second person, present tense, and questions are fired straight at them mid-letter without waiting for an answer — 7/10 samples (`chekhov-056`: *"How have you settled the summer villa question? Is the mongoose alive?"*; `chekhov-132`: *"And you, why are you depressed? What are you depressed about?"*).

Distance is set by a mock-formal or collective vocative used in place of the name — 5/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"My dear Tunguses!"*; `chekhov-043`: *"and no wonder, your Excellency!"*; `chekhov-056`: *"Have you taken a house for the holidays, signori?"*).

Complaint about the recipient is made and then cancelled inside the same sentence — 3/10 samples (`chekhov-056`: *"I made up my mind to pay you out, not to write to you at all--but there, God bless you!"*; `chekhov-073`: *"but you were offended. Well, I can't help it"*). Reproduce it by writing the grievance in full and revoking it with three or four words, not by softening the grievance.

## 5. Figures

Images are made of animals, food, and household bodies — 7/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"she eats mechanically, as though she were munching oats"*, and the steam-tugs that *"look like some fine young intellectual trying to run away while a plebeian wife, mother-in-law, sister-in-law, and wife's grandmother hold on to his coat-tails"*; `chekhov-132`: *"I am in the condition of a transplanted tree which is hesitating whether to take root or to begin to wither"*).

A figure occupies one sentence and is never reopened; the sentence after it has already changed subject — 6/10 samples (`chekhov-001`: the reader's *"unpleasant sensation which mortals feel after eating too many raisins or currants"* is followed immediately by *"Read 'Don Quixote.'"*).

Exaggeration is delivered as plain fact, with nothing marking it as exaggeration — 4/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"They are born in the local iron foundries, and at their birth a mechanic officiates instead of an accoucheur."*; `chekhov-056`: *"Even Vesuvius is so touched it has gone out."*).

## 6. What moves, and what moves with it

Subject sets the register, and eight of the ten letters fall clearly on one side of the split — 8/10 samples. Travel and domestic letters run on physical nouns and short verdicts (`chekhov-031`: *"Kostroma is a nice town."*); letters that argue a case run longer and take abstract nouns as subjects (`chekhov-115`: *"I don't believe in our educated class, which is hypocritical, false, hysterical, badly educated and indolent."*). The remaining two, `chekhov-001` and `chekhov-132`, do both inside one letter.

Illness, weather and money are pitched at exactly the same level as literary judgement — no lift, no apology for the change of subject — 5/10 samples (`chekhov-154` moves from *"my legs no longer ache, I have no diarrhoea, I am beginning to get fat"* to *"One feels there is not a single drop of talent in anything"* within the same letter).

When the recipient is being corrected the sentences shorten into imperatives and drop all scene-setting — 4/10 samples (`chekhov-073`: *"Write another twenty stories and send them."*; `chekhov-001`: *"Among men you must be conscious of your dignity."*).

## 7. Structure and emphasis

Structure is carried by the paragraph break, and a new paragraph opens on the name of its new subject — 9/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"Kundasova is travelling with me."*, then later *"Kostroma is a nice town."*). Nothing in the corpus breaks a letter up with a heading, a numbered list, or a bulleted list.

Movement between subjects is made with a short conversational hinge dropped mid-sentence — 6/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"by the way, yesterday for the first time in my life I saw her eating"*; `chekhov-154`: *"Anyway, I have learned here how to feed."*). No paragraph in the corpus opens on a signposting sentence announcing what it is about to do, or on a connective adverb standing alone at the front.

## 8. What this profile could not determine

Every sample is a translation — 10/10 samples. Punctuation density, dash use, italics and paragraph breaks are Garnett's and her compositor's as much as the author's, and no observation above rests on them.

The ellipsis is the sharpest case. Ellipses appear throughout, but overwhelmingly at the start or the end of a paragraph, where they mark the selection's cuts rather than anything the writer did — 9/10 samples (`chekhov-087` opens on *"... Yes, it is nice now in the country"*). Counted separately, ellipses sitting mid-sentence between two lowercase words — the only ones that cannot be an omission mark — occur in 2/10 samples (`chekhov-031`: *"Rain, cold, mud ... brrr!"*). Do not treat trailing dots as this voice's signature; on this evidence they are mostly the edition's.

Everything above is derived from the corpus alone — 10/10 samples — because `voice.md` is a set of deliberate placeholders with no content to corroborate or contradict.

All ten recipients are family, friends, or correspondents already mid-exchange — 10/10 samples — so nothing here shows how the voice behaves toward a stranger, or in anything written to be published.

The corpus spans 1876 to 1904, but only one sample predates 1888 — 1/10 samples — so an early voice cannot be separated from a late one here.

Every sample is a letter — 10/10 samples. The profile says nothing about how this writer's prose behaves in any other form.
