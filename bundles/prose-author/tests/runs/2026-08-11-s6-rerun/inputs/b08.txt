Overdraft fees are a poverty tax with a customer-service voice.

Here's the part the industry would rather you skipped over: the fee isn't paid by everyone a little, it's paid by a few people constantly. Most people with a checking account won't pay one this year. A thin slice of accounts pay them ten and twenty and thirty times over, and it's that slice — people whose balance lives within a few dollars of zero, which is to say people who are poor — that supplies the whole line item. When your bank calls overdraft protection a "convenience," what it means is that you've been conveniently charged thirty-five dollars for a four-dollar coffee.

Think about a diner that lets you run a tab. You come in through the week: coffee for three bucks on Monday, a sandwich for nine on Tuesday, eggs for six on Wednesday, and then on Thursday you order the steak, forty dollars, and there's only thirty-five in the account behind the tab. One order you couldn't cover. One problem, one penalty. Now imagine the guy at the till has a better idea: before he settles anything up, he re-sorts your tickets biggest to smallest, rings the steak first, watches it swallow your thirty-five dollars, and then announces, with real regret, that the coffee bounced, the sandwich bounced, and the eggs bounced too. Same food, same money, same order you actually placed them in. Four penalties instead of one.

That trick has a name: "high-to-low" posting, or "resequencing" if you're the one doing it and you'd like it to sound like an engineering decision rather than a stickup. It was not a fringe practice. It was standard at the biggest banks in the country, written into the batch job that ran overnight while you slept, and it worked exactly the way the diner does, because the penalty attaches to the transaction that fails rather than to the dollars that are actually missing. Let me put a number beside the practice: in 2010 a federal judge in San Francisco, William Alsup, found that Wells Fargo had reordered debit-card transactions high to low and ordered the bank to return $203 million to its California customers.

The defense, offered with a straight face, was that you preferred it: your rent and your car payment are the big items, and surely you'd want those clearing before your coffee does. It's bullshit, and I want to be precise about why. Nothing about paying the mortgage first requires bouncing everything behind it — a bank that actually cared could clear the big item and decline the small ones for free, which is what a declined card is, free. The sort order wasn't chosen to protect your rent. It was chosen because it produces the largest number of failures per dollar of shortfall, and the "customer preference" story is something somebody pulled out of his ass in a deposition.

So why did it run for decades? Because it worked, and because we didn't stop it. Overdraft was pulling billions of dollars a year out of the thinnest accounts in the system, which meant it was never a feature the banks could be talked out of at a conference — it was a revenue line their earnings guidance sat on. The rule that finally arrived in 2010 went after consent rather than arithmetic: banks had to get you to opt in to debit-card overdraft, so they ran opt-in campaigns, and millions of us opted in to a thing we'd have paid money to avoid. And the people paying were the least able to hire a lawyer, the least likely to be believed at the branch, and the most likely to be told the real problem was their own budgeting: to him that hath shall be given.

What I keep coming back to is how easily it ends when someone decides it should. Capital One dropped overdraft fees entirely in 2021, and Ally dropped them the same year, and both banks are still standing. Go pull your last twelve months of statements and add up what you've been charged for the crime of being short. If there's a number there at all, move your account. But don't let anyone sell you the word again: the convenience was never yours. It was theirs — the convenience of revenue that arrives on its own, on a schedule set by people who will never have to look you in the face, from customers who can't afford to leave.
