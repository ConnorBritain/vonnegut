A streaming service's "buy" button is a lie.

When you click it you aren't buying a film, you're buying a revocable licence to watch a film on hardware whose keys you don't hold, from a company that has itself only rented that film from a studio that will rent it to somebody else the second the contract runs out — and none of that is printed on the button, which says "Buy" in the same friendly typeface a hardware store puts on its "Pay Here" sign.

Think about the hardware store, because the comparison is the whole argument. You hand your money over at the till, you carry the ladder out to your car, and from that second the store's arrangements with its suppliers are none of your business: that's what a sale is, an irreversible transfer that leaves the seller's problems on the seller's side of the counter. If the distributor falls out with the manufacturer, if the licence on the brand name expires, if the whole chain goes bankrupt overnight, the ladder is still in your garage in the morning.

Now put the clerk in your driveway on a Tuesday with a clipboard, explaining that the store's arrangement with the ladder people has lapsed: the ladder goes back, and you'll be getting a credit toward a future ladder, redeemable only there. You'd call the police. You wouldn't hunt through the receipt for the clause that made it legal, because the difference between buying a thing and borrowing it isn't subtle and we've all known it since we were about six.

That clerk is what happens on your television, minus the driveway. Everything you paid for sits in what the service calls "your library": their library, on their servers, tied to an account they can close, and your ownership of it is a row in a database that a lawyer in another building deletes when a five-year window shuts. The windows shut constantly — rights lapse, catalogues get shuffled between services, a studio works out that its back catalogue earns more on its own platform — and one morning the row goes: the film, your place in it, and the money.

The industry's answer, when anyone bothers to ask, is that you knew: it was in the terms of service, clause 4(c), the one you agreed to by breathing. It's bullshit. A contract written by one party and handed over take-it-or-leave-it isn't knowledge, and everybody in the building knows it, which is exactly why the word on the button is "Buy" and not "revocable licence". They know what that word does to a person's expectations. That's why they chose it.

None of this is new, and the canonical case is so on the nose I'd be embarrassed to have invented it: in 2009 Amazon reached into thousands of Kindles and deleted copies of *Nineteen Eighty-Four* that people had paid for. The company apologised and called it a mistake, and I don't think it was lying about that. The capability didn't go anywhere, though. Any shelf a company can fill overnight is a shelf it can empty overnight, and the only thing standing between us and that is a running estimate of how much bad press the emptying would cost.

So the question isn't whether they'll do it: it's what we're left holding when they do. Buy the disc while discs exist, and take the download wherever a shop still hands you a file that plays without phoning a server for permission. Those shops are worse in almost every respect — worse apps, worse search, no glowing wall of posters — and they're the only ones whose collapse next year doesn't take the collection with it.

And we should stop treating this as something we can shop our way out of, because the reason the button lies is that lying on it has been free. California decided it shouldn't be, and told storefronts they can't say "buy" for a digital good without saying plainly that it can be taken back. It's a good law and a narrow one: it makes the seller describe the trapdoor accurately and leaves the trapdoor where it is. "Your library" is still a phrase that came out of a marketing department the way a hairball comes out of a cat.

Because the trapdoor is the product. A shelf we can't lose is a shelf nobody gets to sell us twice, and every incentive runs the other way: the collection that empties is the collection you restock, at their price, on their schedule, for as long as you care about films at all. Nobody ever had to restock a ladder. The button was honest about one thing. Somebody was buying something, and it wasn't you.
