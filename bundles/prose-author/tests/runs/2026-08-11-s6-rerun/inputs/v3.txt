The security advice goes like this: your memory is the weak link, so take it out of the loop. Get a password manager, let it mint sixty characters of line noise for every site you touch, switch on two-factor, and never type the same string twice. It's good advice. I've given it to my mother, to coworkers, to strangers at parties who find out what I do and want one thing they can go home and fix that evening. Every word of it holds at the level of a single account, and a single account is no longer where the interesting failures happen.

Picture a locksmith on your high street: a counter, a till, a wall of blanks, a man who cuts you a spare for six quid and hands it over still warm. He's better at locks than you are — he knows which cylinders are junk and which will survive a teenager with a screwdriver, and if you let him pick your front door hardware you'd come out ahead of whatever you'd have grabbed off a peg yourself. Nothing about him is a scam. That's the part people get wrong when they hear this argument coming.

But he keeps a copy. Not out of malice: he keeps it so that when you lock yourself out at eleven at night he can bring you a spare instead of drilling your door out of its frame at your expense. It lives in a box under the till, and the box is a good box, and he is careful with it. Now let every house on the street use him, then the next street, then the county, because he really is the best and because the alternative is your memory, which is the weak link, as everyone keeps telling you.

The box under the till is now the most valuable object for a hundred miles: one burglary, one night, one small business with one employee who clicks one thing, and the burglar owns every front door in the county. The quality of the box has stopped being the question. And the burglar isn't in a hurry, because a copied key keeps working for years while you sleep soundly on advice you were handed a decade ago and have never had cause to revisit.

That's the industry. LastPass had customer vault backups stolen, and the encrypted copies left with the attacker, who has all the time in the world and none of our urgency: no rate limits, no lockouts, no alarm on the door, just a copy of the box and years of cheap compute to point at it. The vendors that survived that story came out of it with more customers, not fewer.

That's what a breach does in a market shaped like this one: it kills the small players and fattens the survivors. A handful of companies now hold the keys for most of the corporate world — one throat to choke — and the browsers hold the rest by default, and every one of them describes itself with the same two words: "zero knowledge", as though an architecture diagram were a promise. It's a real property doing real work. But offered as the reason the concentration doesn't matter, it's bullshit: zero knowledge stops your vendor from reading your vault, not from being the one address on earth worth robbing.

Meanwhile the advice hasn't moved an inch. It's still "use a password manager", still "don't reuse passwords", still filed under "user error" and "password hygiene", still aimed squarely at the one participant in this system with no leverage — you — while the question of what happens when four vaults hold everything goes unasked. We told a billion people to put every key they own into the same kind of box, and then congratulated ourselves on the strength of the box. We built one key to rule them all and shipped it as a convenience feature.

So do the boring things the advice should have told you years ago. Print your recovery codes and put them somewhere physical. Keep your email and your bank out of the vault entirely, in your head, where the blast radius is one person. Export twice a year, because the company holding your keys can lose you as easily as it can lose them, and a firm that gets acquired at four in the morning owes you nothing by lunchtime.

None of that fixes the shape of the problem, and I'm not pretending it does. The fix is structural: interoperability, so that leaving a vendor costs you an afternoon instead of your whole digital life, and liability, so that losing ten million vaults costs more than a blog post and a year of credit monitoring. Until then we're all standing in the same shop, at the same counter, being told that the weak link is our memory. The weak link was never your memory. It's the box under the till.
