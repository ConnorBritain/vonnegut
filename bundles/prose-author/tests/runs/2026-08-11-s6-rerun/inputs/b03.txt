Parts pairing is the most elegant swindle in modern manufacturing.

Here's how it works: something in your device fails, you buy the replacement — an identical component, out of the same bin at the same factory — you fit it, and the machine won't take it. Nothing is wrong with it. There's a number in the old part and a matching number in the firmware, and when the two stop agreeing, software you paid for and don't control decides your repair didn't happen: an error, a permanent nag screen, or the feature you bought the thing for quietly switched off. Somebody at the company has to run a tool and bless the new part before your device will admit it's there.

Run that at the cobbler's. You bring in boots with the soles gone through. He has soles — the same leather, from the same tannery that supplied the boot company — and a knife, a last, and thirty years of knowing where to cut. He does the work in an hour. Then he phones the manufacturer, reads out the number stamped in the new sole, and waits, because the rule is simple: until someone three states away types that number into a form, your boots are not boots.

What gets me isn't the wait. It's what the wait does to the shop: the boot company never has to refuse him, it only has to be slow, or charge for the tool, or sell the form as a subscription, or decide his shop is too small to qualify. None of that is a ban on repair. He didn't get worse at cobbling. The work moved behind a form he can't fill in, and we have all watched a trade die this way.

That's the tractor, the phone, the wheelchair and the ice cream machine: one lock in different housings, a bouncer in the guts of the machine checking IDs. John Deere built an empire on the premise that a farmer with a wrench is a threat, and that the only person who may tell a machine costing more than a house that its new sensor is really its new sensor is a dealer technician who bills by the hour. Apple spent years telling people who'd fitted a real Apple screen that the phone was "unable to verify" it. The screen was as real as the one it replaced. What it lacked was a number the phone would accept.

The answer, when you ask why, is always the same three words: "safety", "security", "counterfeit". It's bullshit. I've been listening to that argument for a decade and I've yet to hear it applied to a part that was actually dangerous, because pairing doesn't test the part, it tests the paperwork. A cell that fails every safety standard passes if it carries the right number, and a perfect one pulled from an identical handset fails. The check runs against a database, not against the battery, and we're the ones told it's for our own good.

We borrowed a word from the theologians for a rite that changes what a thing is while changing nothing about it: transubstantiation. The sole is the same sole, the sensor is the same sensor, and then the words are said over it and it becomes "genuine". And because the words are said in software, doing it yourself isn't a repair, it's a circumvention: Section 1201 of the DMCA makes getting around a digital lock a federal offence, so the fix you're entitled to perform is one you can be prosecuted for performing.

Which is why these fights come down to a paragraph most of us never read. Oregon banned parts pairing outright in 2024. Most repair laws leave it standing, and I think that omission is the whole ballgame: you can win the right to buy the part and still be holding a component your device won't acknowledge. Watch what the manufacturers concede and what they don't — the schematic, the tool, even the part, and then they fight to the last lobbyist for the number that has to match.

We aren't asking permission to break our things. We're asking to stop needing permission to fix them — from a company that's already been paid, that isn't in the room, that will one day stop answering at all, and when it does, the sole and the screen and the sensor will still be on the shelf, still perfectly good, and still not genuine.
