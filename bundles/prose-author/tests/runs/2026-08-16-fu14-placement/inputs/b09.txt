Unpredictable hours are a pay cut that never shows up on your pay stub.

Here's what happens while you're asleep: a workforce-management system — Kronos, or any of the dozen products that do the same job under a friendlier name — reads last year's foot traffic against tomorrow's forecast, the high school's football calendar and the register data from the last eleven Tuesdays, and decides at 9pm on Thursday whether you work Saturday, and whether the shift ends at eleven or gets cut at 8:15 because the evening came in soft. You find out when your phone buzzes. The math has been running for weeks, and you're the only party to it who never got to see the numbers.

Think about what the store you work in is genuinely good at: holding things it doesn't need yet. It keeps milk it hasn't sold, receipt paper it hasn't printed on, a back room of seasonal stock bought six months before anyone will want it, and it pays rent on that room, and it eats the loss on whatever spoils: a store that orders milk only after a customer asks for milk isn't a store. Holding things is the price of being open. Every retailer on earth has made peace with that, which is why the back room all of us have walked past exists at all, and why the milk is there on the one evening you want it.

Labor is the one input we've agreed they don't have to warehouse. The scheduler's whole job is to buy your hour at the moment it's needed and not a minute sooner — the euphemism is "demand-driven scheduling" — except that the thing arriving just in time is a person, and a person has a lease, a kid and a bus that comes every forty minutes. The uncertainty didn't go anywhere. It moved onto you, unpaid, and the industry calls what's left "flexibility."

So you keep Wednesday open. Not for anything: in case. You can't take the second job, because the second job wants to know when you're free and the first one won't say until Thursday night. You can't enroll in the class that meets Tuesdays and Thursdays, you can't tell the daycare what time you're coming, and when your hours land at 19 one week and 34 the next, you can't tell the landlord anything at all — the rent is predictable and everything you'd pay it with isn't. Then the good shifts go to whoever kept the most days open, so the reward for having a life is the ass end of the week, and none of that shows up anywhere in the model.

There's an old story about food that appears on the ground every morning and rots if you try to keep any of it overnight. It reads two ways: a miracle when it's the desert and God's doing it, a business model when it's your employer. And the answer we get every time is the same one — that people in these jobs want the flexibility, that a fixed schedule would be a cage. That's horseshit. I've never once heard it from anyone whose own Tuesday wasn't protected by an assistant, a calendar and a locked door.

The vocabulary gives it away: "clopening" is the word for closing at midnight and opening at five, an arrangement nobody would propose to a person they had to see the next day. We have a name for the "on-call shift" too, which should tell us something, because it's the one where you organize your entire day around work that may not happen and that you aren't paid for. Somebody had to coin these, which means somebody read the output, understood what it was, and shipped it anyway.

We've started, in a few places, to send the holding cost back where it came from. Fair workweek laws in Seattle, New York, San Francisco, Philadelphia and Chicago, plus a statewide one in Oregon, mostly do the same few things: give you the schedule two weeks out, pay you "predictability pay" when it changes late, pay you something when an on-call shift is cancelled, and let you refuse the clopening. Look up whether your city has one, because in most of them enforcement is a complaint you file yourself. I don't think a fourteen-day notice rule fixes a labor market and I'm not going to pretend it does. It does something narrower: it puts a price on uncertainty, and software that has to pay for uncertainty starts producing schedules that look a lot more like a week.

That's the part I'd hold onto. Nobody at head office thinks uncertainty is free: they've priced it into the milk, the receipt paper and the back room. They found exactly one input that would absorb it for nothing — you — and it works because we let the cost land in your kitchen instead of their warehouse. The flexibility was never yours. You're the inventory.
