The "Buy" button on a streaming store is a lie, and everyone shipping it knows it. You click it, your card gets charged roughly what a disc would have cost, the film shows up in a shelf-shaped corner of the app called "Your Library," and every one of those decisions — the verb, the price, the shelf — is doing the work of persuading you that something has crossed the counter from their side to yours. It hasn't. What crossed was a permission slip: revocable, term-limited, and governed by a clause you agreed to at eleven at night on a phone you were holding over your face in bed.

Imagine buying a chainsaw this way. You pay at the till, the clerk hands it over, and then he mentions the arrangement the store has with the manufacturer: when the arrangement lapses, a van comes for the chainsaw, and no, there's no refund, because what you bought was never a chainsaw but an indefinite and now-concluded loan of one. You wouldn't stand for it in a hardware store. You wouldn't stand for it from a bike shop, a bookshop, or the man who sold you your mattress, and if the mattress man tried it, we'd have a word for him and it wouldn't be "retailer." The only thing that makes a film different is that a film can be repossessed without anyone having to drive to your house.

Here's the mechanism, and it isn't complicated: the service never owned the film either. It licensed it from a studio for a term, and the term covering the copies it sold you is frequently the same term covering the ones it rents by the night. When that term runs out, the studio stops answering, and the DRM wrapped around your copy becomes a lock with the key on the wrong side of the door. Your entry in "Your Library" doesn't grey out. It vanishes. Sony did this to PlayStation Store customers over Discovery shows, warning them that purchased content would disappear from their libraries; Microsoft did it to everyone who'd bought an ebook from its store, and had at least the decency to refund them.

Then comes the part that turns a bad deal into a trap: it is illegal for you to save yourself. Section 1201 of the DMCA makes it a federal crime to break a digital lock, even a lock on a thing you paid for, even a lock whose owner has wandered off and left it clamped to your property. You may not strip the DRM and keep the file. You may not pay someone to do it for you. You may not pass on the tool that would do it. The law that would let you keep the copy you bought is the same law guaranteeing you can't, which isn't a bug in the DMCA but the entirety of its job.

And there's nobody to shout at. The notice, when it comes, is written in the passive: titles "will no longer be available" owing to "licensing restrictions," and the company "appreciates your understanding." No human decided to reach into your house; a term expired, in a contract between two parties, neither of whom is you. This is the corporation with no soul to damn and no body to kick, except now it holds your receipts too. It's bullshit, and it's bullshit we have collectively agreed to call "buying."

I keep a shelf of DVDs I don't especially want, for one reason: the same reason anyone keeps a fire extinguisher. I'm not sentimental about discs, either — a disc is a scratchable, mislayable nuisance that needs a machine nobody's shipped in a laptop for a decade. But the nuisance is mine. Buy the disc when the thing genuinely matters to you, because the money is the same and the ownership is real, and if the studio wants it back it will have to come and ask.

The pitch for the streaming library was simple: we'd stop paying for objects and start paying for access, and access would be so cheap and so complete that nobody would miss the objects. What we got instead is objects priced like objects and delivered like access, with the risk of the licence resting on the customer — which is where this industry has been quietly moving risk for twenty years, whether the thing at the other end is a film, a shift, or a pension. Our libraries are collections of other people's promises, and promises expire on a schedule we don't get shown. The button says Buy, and the button is the only part of that transaction we own.
