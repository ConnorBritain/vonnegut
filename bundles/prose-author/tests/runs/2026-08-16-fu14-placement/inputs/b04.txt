The pitch goes like this: the industry has heard you, it's sorry about the last twenty years, and it has been building something better. The processing happens "on the device." Nothing identifying leaves your phone. You get sorted into a "cohort" rather than a file, noise is sprinkled over the aggregate so that no single person can be pulled back out of it, and somebody with a mathematics degree signs off on the whole arrangement. And here's the part that's meant to make you exhale: none of it costs the advertiser a thing, because the targeting is going to get *better*. More accurate and less invasive, at once, as though those were two dials on opposite walls of the room.

It's one dial. Accuracy in advertising has one meaning and has never had another — the ability to tell you apart from the person standing next to you at the bus stop. That's what the buyer is buying: not "relevance," not "signal," but a partition of the human race fine enough that you land on one side of it and your neighbour lands on the other, cut at whatever resolution somebody is willing to pay for. Invasiveness is just the word we use for that same resolution when we're the ones being resolved.

Picture the pharmacy on the corner, bell over the door, shoebox of index cards behind the counter. The pharmacist knows who's on lithium, who came in at eleven at night, whose husband collects the prescription so she doesn't have to walk in herself. Now suppose he explains that he's found a way to sell that knowledge to the sleep-aid rep without breaching anyone's confidence: the cards never leave the shop, he'll simply tell the rep which shelf to stock, which hours to send the sample bag round, and which four customers to hand the coupon to. He hasn't disclosed a thing. He's disclosed everything.

That's the whole on-device story. The data stays put and the inference walks out the door, and the inference is what the buyer wanted in the first place — nobody ever wanted your browsing history, they wanted the conclusion it supports. Keeping the shoebox behind the counter while shipping the conclusion is not privacy: it's the monkey's-paw version of privacy, the wish granted exactly as worded and in no other respect.

Which is why the fights over Google's Privacy Sandbox, over Topics, over Apple's tracking prompt have all run the same course. A scheme gets proposed. Buyers test it, find the cohorts too coarse to price a bid against, and complain — in the trade press, in the standards meetings, and in the phrase "signal loss," which repays a moment's attention: "signal" is what they call us when they're talking among themselves. Then the coarseness gets sanded down, revision by revision, until the numbers come back where the buyers want them. Performing means resolving you, and the sanding has never once run in the other direction.

So when someone tells you a system hits the same targets while knowing less, ask what "less" is measured against, over what window, and whether the comparison is to last year's machine or to not running one. I'll grant them a single hedge — some of this is genuine engineering, done by people who mean it. The claim built on top of it came out of the same orifice as every other promise this industry has made about itself, and it's bullshit for a reason that has nothing to do with anyone's sincerity: there is no configuration of the thing in which it aims better at us and sees less of us.

There is a real version of less invasive, and everyone in the business knows what it is, which is why you'll notice it never headlines the announcements: you sell the page, not the person. A recipe site sells the attention of someone reading about braising, a repair forum sells the attention of someone with a broken dishwasher, and none of us has to know whose dishwasher it is or what else in that house is failing. That's a genuine reduction. It's also a genuine reduction in accuracy, and the trade is legible to every party making it.

So when the next deck lands in my inbox promising both at once, understand what's on offer. You aren't being sold a better machine. You're being sold a better word for the machine you've already got, and they can turn the dial toward you or away from you, and they'll happily tell you which way they've turned it, and the thing they will never say out loud is that there was only ever the one dial.
