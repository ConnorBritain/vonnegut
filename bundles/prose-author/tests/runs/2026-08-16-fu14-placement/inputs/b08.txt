Overdraft fees are a tax on being poor, collected by people who know it. That isn't a complaint about fairness, it's a description of where the money comes from: the accounts that pay overdraft fees are overwhelmingly the accounts that live near zero all month, belonging to people who get paid Friday and need groceries Wednesday, and the regulators' own numbers have said so for a decade — a small minority of checking accounts generate the large majority of the fees, over and over, ten and twelve and twenty times a year, because a $35 charge against an account holding $8 doesn't make the next overdraft less likely, it makes it certain. If you've never paid one, you weren't the customer. You were the loss leader.

The industry story is that you spent money you didn't have and the fee is the price of a "courtesy": the bank covered your rent when your balance couldn't. Sometimes that's true. But the first time I had the actual mechanism explained to me I assumed it was an exaggeration, because for most of the 2000s the big banks weren't waiting around to see whether you'd overdraw — they were arranging your day so that you would, and so that you'd do it four times instead of once. The trick has a name, "high-to-low," and it works like this.

Say you walk into a corner shop with $40 and a basket: a $38 turkey, a quart of milk, a pack of gum, a birthday card, a box of batteries. The cashier could ring the five things in the order you set them on the counter, in which case four of them clear and only the turkey comes up short. Instead he rings the turkey first, drains your $40 down to $2, and then rings the milk, the gum, the card and the batteries in turn, charging you $35 apiece, because each one — at the moment he chose to ring it — was money you didn't have. Same basket. Same forty dollars. One shortfall became four, and the shop cleared $140 on the ordering alone.

That is what banks did with debit transactions, at scale, sorting each day's charges largest-to-smallest before posting them rather than posting them in the order you made them. When customers sued, the defense was "customer preference" — people want the mortgage and the car note to clear first, so high-to-low was a service to the account holder. It's bullshit. Wells Fargo made that argument in a federal courtroom in California and lost, and the judge found the sorting had been engineered to multiply the number of fees rather than to protect anyone's rent, and ordered $203 million returned.

So why did it last twenty years? Partly because the harm is atomized: $140 taken from you in an afternoon is a catastrophe in your kitchen and a rounding error on an earnings call, and the people it lands on are the least able to hire a lawyer and the most likely to have signed away their right to join a class. Partly because "free checking" was sold as free and quietly paid for by the overdrafters, which gave everyone with a comfortable balance a stake in not looking too closely. And partly because the fix, when it arrived in 2010, was an opt-in — banks had to get your permission first, and a great many of them got it, by asking whether you'd like your card to keep working.

That last part is what we keep getting wrong about consumer protection. We treat a disclosure as a cure and we treat a form signed at a counter as a decision made freely, when the person signing has a gut sense that saying no means the card gets refused at the pharmacy in front of the pharmacist. Consent extracted there isn't consent, it's a fee schedule with a signature line. Go find the posting-order clause in your own account agreement — it's four sentences long, and it tells you whether your bank still reserves the right to sort your day.

Things have improved, and I don't think that's only regulators: Capital One dropped overdraft fees outright, Ally dropped them, Bank of America cut its fee from $35 to $10, and high-to-low reordering on debit transactions has largely collapsed. Billions a year that used to come out of accounts like yours no longer do. But the fee still exists at most institutions, the posting rules are still mostly the bank's to write, and every one of those improvements is a policy somebody chose and can unchoose the quarter fee income needs to come back.

Which is the thing to hold onto when you're told the market handled this. The market ran it for two decades, and it ran it because sorting a list of numbers is the cheapest revenue on earth and because the people paying couldn't afford to walk. The fee was never a courtesy the bank extended to you when your balance ran short. It was a courtesy the bank extended to itself, out of your account, in an order it picked.
