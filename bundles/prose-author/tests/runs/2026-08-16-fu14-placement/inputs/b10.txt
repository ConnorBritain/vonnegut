A gadget that phones home is a rental with a purchase price. You didn't lease it, you weren't quoted a term, and nobody at the register mentioned the actual shape of the deal: the thing in the box keeps working exactly as long as some vice-president four states away finds the hosting bill tolerable — which is the arrangement you entered when you bought a doorbell, a thermostat, a car, or a fridge with a screen in the door. The hardware is yours, the service isn't, and the working life of your property is now a line item in a stranger's budget, filed between the office lease and the coffee.

Move it into a shop with a counter and a till and it gets easier to see. You buy a deadbolt, you pay cash, the guy behind the counter drops it in a bag and thanks you. Then he mentions one last thing on your way out: the bolt works because the store keeps a copy of your key in the back, and every time you turn the handle the lock calls the store to ask whether you're still allowed inside. You wouldn't buy that lock. Nobody would, because a lock whose function depends on a stranger staying in business isn't a lock, it's a favour, and the store decides how long it feels like doing you one.

But we do buy that lock. We buy it by the pallet, because the part where it calls the store is invisible at the moment of sale and the price on the shelf is the only number the transaction shows you. The store doesn't close dramatically, either. It sends an email. The email is warm: it thanks you for being "part of the journey", it uses the word "sunset" as though the thing had gone gently into the west, and four paragraphs down it explains that in six weeks the lock on your front door will be a lump of brass.

None of this is hypothetical and the names aren't hard to come by: Google bought Revolv, a smart home hub, switched off the servers, and the hubs went dark in the houses of everyone who'd bought one. Spotify sold a dashboard music player called Car Thing and then bricked it, and the disposal instructions for the device you paid for came down to the bin. Chamberlain cut off the third-party apps people were using to open their own garage doors. BMW sold heated seats — wire already in the seat, seat already in the car, all of it already paid for — as a monthly subscription. Each of those was a slide in a deck with somebody's name on it, and the room where it got approved has never once contained one of us.

I want to be exact about the part that makes me furious: it isn't that servers go away. Servers cost money and companies fold, and I don't think that's a conspiracy so much as arithmetic. It's that when the server goes, the device is still perfectly good hardware — a radio, a processor, a motor, a heating element, all of it fine — and someone in a forum somewhere has already worked out how to point it at a replacement server they run themselves, and the moment you do that you hit one law: Section 1201 of the DMCA, which makes getting past a digital lock a felony no matter what you own and no matter what you paid. The law we were sold as protection for songwriters is the reason your dead thermostat has to stay dead. It's bullshit. They would simply rather you bought a new one, and 1201 is how that preference gets to be the law.

And notice how it gets described — not as a decision but as weather. The product "reached end of life", as though it had a life, as though it hadn't been strangled in a meeting by people who then went to lunch. Things that have lifespans: dogs, horses, us. Things that have termination dates: leases, contracts, and apparently your front door. I have yet to see one of these emails use the word "killed", and the six weeks in them is not an engineering constraint but a number somebody pulled out of his arse.

So ask it at the counter, out loud, before the money moves: what happens to this when you stop paying to run the servers? Nobody there will be able to tell you, and the fact that nobody can tell you is the answer. We have a name for a thing you pay for once and use until it wears out: property. What's in the box isn't that, it's a rental at a purchase price, with the term left blank so the landlord can fill it in later, and he will.
