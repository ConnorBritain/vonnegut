A six-month trial can leave a much longer reading record. In this fictional scenario, a town library is considering a reading app whose proposed contract permits the vendor to retain identifiable reading histories for two years after a patron closes an account. The library hasn't signed.

That clock deserves attention: the two years begin when the account closes. A patron could finish using the app and still leave a record linking their identity to their reading (a remarkably persistent souvenir).

Imagine you're considering a book about a subject you'd prefer to explore privately. Knowing that your choice could remain attached to your identity might make you hesitate. You might choose something less revealing, postpone the inquiry, or abandon it altogether. The possibility of later scrutiny could become part of deciding what to read.

That's a risk argument. Nothing in this scenario establishes that a patron has changed their reading or that anyone has accessed a retained history. The concern is that this arrangement could make private inquiry feel like browsing a shop where the proprietor keeps a named ledger of your selections long after you've left. The vendor would hold the record; the reader would carry the worry.

The proposed alternative is concrete: retain only anonymous aggregate usage totals and let patrons read without creating an account. Totals would describe usage collectively. Reading without an account would let a patron open a book without first establishing an account identity. Both provisions belong in the proposed contract revision.
