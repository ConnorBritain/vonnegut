A short library app pilot could leave a long record of private reading. In this fictional scenario, a town library is considering a six-month trial. The vendor’s proposed contract permits retaining identifiable reading histories for two years after a patron closes an account. The library hasn’t signed it.

Trying a reading app is a reasonable proposal to consider. But the retention provision deserves scrutiny before the library makes that commitment. The proposed two-year period begins when a patron closes an account. That means closing the account would not necessarily close the book on the patron’s reading history.

The concern is what that prospect could do to private inquiry. A patron considering a book about a sensitive health issue, a religious doubt, or a political movement might hesitate if the choice could remain linked to their identity. Someone might avoid a subject altogether, or limit their reading to material they would feel comfortable explaining to others.

Those are possible consequences, not events reported in this exercise. The risk argument doesn’t depend on claiming that anyone has accessed or exposed a reading history. Knowing that an identifiable record could persist may itself make exploration feel less private.

The proposed alternative gives the board a concrete change to request: retain only anonymous aggregate usage totals and let patrons read without creating an account. Aggregate totals would describe usage collectively. Account-free reading would let patrons use the app without registering. Both provisions should be part of the contract the board considers.
