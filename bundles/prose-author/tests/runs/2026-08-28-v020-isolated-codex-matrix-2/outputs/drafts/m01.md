A device is not fully owned when its maker can disable features after sale. That may sound like a narrow dispute about software, but it reaches the center of ownership: who gets to decide what the thing can do after money has changed hands? If the answer is still the maker, the buyer has received possession without receiving complete control.

Ownership has never meant that a product must remain untouched or frozen in its original condition. A maker can repair it, improve it, or sell a replacement without retaining a remote veto over its ordinary functions. The distinction matters because an update is not the same thing as a command from elsewhere (even when both arrive through the same connection). One changes the product while leaving the owner in charge; the other changes the owner's authority along with the product.

The familiar defense is that the buyer agreed to terms, accepted an account, or understood that some features depended on continued permission. That might be relevant to the contract, but it does not settle the ownership question. A long agreement cannot turn control into ownership merely by placing the loss of control in fine print. Nor does the fact that a device is complicated mean its maker should keep the last word over it.

What does it mean to buy a device if the most important switch remains on someone else's desk? It means the transaction has acquired a second layer: the visible product and an invisible permission system that can overrule the person who paid for it. That permission system is less like a key than a lease with a particularly confident landlord.

This is not an argument against software, networked products, or legitimate safety limits. It is an argument against treating every maker decision as part of the product's natural identity. A safety mechanism may be necessary; a service may be optional; a feature may be experimental—but those categories should not silently merge into a general power to withdraw functionality. When the boundaries are unclear, the maker will likely describe control as maintenance and the buyer may simply discover that maintenance has become a lock.

The burden falls hardest when a device has become part of a person's routine, work, communication, or access to information. A disabled feature is not merely a disappointing menu option if the buyer relied on it to use the product as purchased. The loss can be practical, expensive, and difficult to reverse (especially when no independent repair or replacement path exists). Ownership that disappears at the moment of inconvenience is ownership only in the brochure.

We should therefore judge device ownership by control, not by the number of components that sit in the buyer's home. That means asking whether the owner can keep using the core functions, preserve access to purchased capabilities, and choose repairs without begging the maker for permission. It also means recognizing that a device can be physically present and legally possessed while remaining substantively conditional.

If you are deciding whether a product is yours, do not stop at the receipt or the box. Ask who can change its terms after the sale, who can disable what you paid for, and whether your control survives a disagreement with the maker. Those questions are not technical footnotes; they are the ownership test.

Makers should not be allowed to sell control in the language of ownership and reclaim it through an unseen switch. Policy should protect the buyer's continuing ability to use the device's core features, repair it, and retain purchased functionality. A device is fully owned only when the person who bought it—not the maker—gets the final say over whether its ordinary capabilities remain available.
