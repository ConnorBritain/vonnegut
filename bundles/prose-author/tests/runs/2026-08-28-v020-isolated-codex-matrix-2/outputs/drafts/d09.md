“Confidential” is doing a lot of work in this proposal, so let’s make the decision plain: don’t put identifiable intake notes into a general-purpose AI service for tomorrow’s meeting. If you can’t explain, in one sentence, who may retain the text, who may inspect it, whether it’s used for training, and how you’d delete it, you don’t have a safe process yet. That isn’t anti-AI; it’s pro-client.

You can still test the workflow with invented notes or text that’s been stripped so thoroughly that nobody could reconnect it to a person (names, dates, locations, rare circumstances, all of it). But be honest about what that proves. It proves that a tool can produce a summary from a safe sample, not that the tool belongs anywhere near the real files.

Think of the service as an escrow agent – it’s holding something valuable while promising to pass back something useful. You wouldn’t hand an escrow agent your clients’ stories because its logo looked trustworthy. You’d read the agreement, identify the custodian, limit the instructions, and decide what happens when the relationship ends. The same standard applies here (even if the interface is friendly and the sales pitch says “privacy”).

For the meeting, bring a simple decision rule: real confidential notes stay out unless the provider has passed your organization’s privacy, security, retention, access, and deletion review. If that review hasn’t happened, use a local, approved tool or do the summaries manually for now. Yes, manual work is slower. So is explaining to a client why their most vulnerable account became training material for somebody else’s machine.

And if someone says the risk is “probably fine,” ask: fine for whom? The nonprofit gets convenience; your client carries the exposure. That’s bullshit accounting, and you should reject it. We’re not trying to make the fanciest summary tomorrow. We’re trying to protect the people who trusted you enough to tell the truth. Until the safeguards are real, the answer is no.
