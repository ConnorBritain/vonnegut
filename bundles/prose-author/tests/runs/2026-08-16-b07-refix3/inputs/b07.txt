You never bought that movie. You bought a promise from a company that kept the right to break it, and one morning the promise lapses and the film slides out of the library you paid for while you're asleep, and the confirmation-email in your inbox goes on insisting, in the cheerful past tense, that the purchase completed.

The chain has three links and you sit at the bottom of it. A studio licenses a catalogue to a platform for a term of years. The platform sublicenses it onward to you and calls what it sold you a purchase, because Buy converts better on a button than Licence Pending Renewal. When the top link lapses, the platform's obligation lapses with it, because the platform never held anything sturdier than what it had itself. Everything under the break falls. That's the design:

In 2023 Sony told PlayStation owners that the Discovery shows they'd bought outright would be deleted from their libraries, with no refund and no replacement, and reversed the decision only after the noise got loud, which is the useful part, because it means the deletion was a choice and not a law of physics. Ultraviolet, the studio consortium that was going to fix all of this with one durable cloud-locker for the digital copies you already owned, closed the locker in 2019.

Funimation's digital copies went the same way into Crunchyroll. Nintendo closed the Wii Shop Channel and the redownload window shut behind it. Amazon reached into people's Kindles in 2009 and deleted their copies of Orwell, which is still the most on-the-nose thing a company has managed. Amazon's terms-of-service have said the quiet part for years: a Prime Video purchase is a licence, revocable, and it always was.

Sure, the fine print always said so. Every one of those documents defines the purchase as a revocable licence, and a lawyer will explain, patiently, that your click on the checkbox was consent. But the button says Buy. It doesn't say Borrow. It doesn't say Until Further Notice, and the company that A/B-tested its way to that button knows precisely which of the three moves the most product. Calling that a meeting of minds is bullshit.

A DVD on a shelf resists this. Nobody drives over and takes it back, not because the studio wouldn't like to, but because repossession in the physical world costs money – and DRM is the technology for driving that cost to zero. The file on your device has a lock on it, and the lock answers to a server that isn't yours. Under Section 1201 of the DMCA, picking that lock is a federal offence even when the thing behind it is yours, so the one self-help move available to you is the move Congress removed in 1998.

So we've had the arrangement backwards. We talk about our libraries – our shelves, the thing we spent fifteen years assembling – but a library that can be emptied from outside isn't a library, it's a tenancy, and the landlord is a rights-holder three companies away who has never heard of us and owes us nothing. Feudal is the word people reach for here, and it earns its keep. The lord holds the land, and the tenant holds his access at the lord's pleasure, and that pleasure is a clause with a renewal date on it. There's one move left to somebody who'd rather keep what he paid for:

Buy the disc in the cases where a disc still exists. Rip it, keep a copy, and put the copy somewhere that phones nobody. That advice doesn't scale and I know it. I own a shelf of discs and a laptop with no optical drive, and the last three things I watched arrived over a wire from a company that can reach back down the wire whenever a contract runs out. My library is somebody's licensing spreadsheet with a nice interface on it.

Own is the word doing the work here, and it means one specific thing: the object stays yours when the people who sold it to you change their minds. A purchase that a licensing calendar can undo doesn't pass that test. A company that can reach into your library and take the thing back never sold it, and calling that transaction a purchase is a marketing decision wearing the clothes of a fact.
