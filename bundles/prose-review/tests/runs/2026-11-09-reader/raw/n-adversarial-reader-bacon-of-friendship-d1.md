**WHERE I STOPPED**: line 86–90, "but being men so wise, of such strength and severity of mind, and so extreme lovers of themselves, as all these were, it proveth most plainly that they found their own felicity (though as great as ever happened to mortal men) but as an half-piece, except they might have a friend to make it entire"
**WHY, AS THIS READER**: The draft's own examples say these favourites overmatched their princes, drew Caesar "forth to his death" and had to be married in or killed, and it admitted earlier that the practice "many times sorteth to inconvenience". So the list shows that powerful men raised dangerous favourites. It does not show "most plainly" that friendship made them whole, and I'd stand up right here.

**WHERE I STOPPED**: line 145–148, "certain it is, that the light that a man receiveth by counsel from another, is drier and purer than that which cometh from his own understanding and judgment, which is ever infused and drenched in his affections and customs"
**WHY, AS THIS READER**: The friend has affections and customs too, and the draft's only answer comes later, at lines 177–179: counsel is bent to the giver's ends "except it be from a perfect and entire friend." That answer is circular. It saves the claim only by defining the good friend as the one whose counsel isn't bent.

**FORCED CHOICE**: "it proveth most plainly that they found their own felicity (though as great as ever happened to mortal men) but as an half-piece, except they might have a friend to make it entire" (lines 88–90). This is where the longest run of evidence is asked to carry the most weight, and that same evidence points the other way.
