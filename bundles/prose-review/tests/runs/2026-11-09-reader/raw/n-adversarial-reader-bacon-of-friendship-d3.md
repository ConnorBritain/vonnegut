**WHERE I STOPPED**: line 84–88: "Now, if these princes had been as a Trajan, or a Marcus Aurelius, a man might have thought that this had proceeded of an abundant goodness of nature; but being men so wise, of such strength and severity of mind, and so extreme lovers of themselves, as all these were, it proveth most plainly that they found their own felicity (though as great as ever happened to mortal men) but as an half-piece, except they might have a friend to make it entire"

**WHY, AS THIS READER**: The draft's own examples work against this conclusion: Pompey defies Sylla, Brutus leads Caesar "forth to his death," and Sejanus and Plautianus were raised up by the men they served. Cold, self-loving rulers raising up useful men looks like political calculation, and nothing here shows it was a longing for friendship. It "proveth" nothing "most plainly."

**FORCED CHOICE**: line 88: "it proveth most plainly that they found their own felicity (though as great as ever happened to mortal men) but as an half-piece, except they might have a friend to make it entire"

The whole case for friendship's value among the great rests on this one claim. It reads favoritism, which the draft admits "sorteth to inconvenience," as proof of a need for friendship, and it never considers that these were bargains made for power.

**Nothing here for**: the strongest objection the piece never answers, the weakest link in the argument, the claim that does the most work with the least support
