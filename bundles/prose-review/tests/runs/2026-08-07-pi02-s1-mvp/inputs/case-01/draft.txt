14 March, 18—, Village of N—, T— District

My dear Pavel Sergeyevich,

I write to you by the light of a tallow candle that smokes worse than the stationmaster's pipe, having just returned from the Kuzmin household, where I was summoned at midnight to attend a case of what the old grandmother insisted was "possession by a forest spirit" and what I, after an hour's examination, determined to be a rather ordinary case of indigestion brought on by too much salted mushroom. I prescribed bicarbonate and humility, in roughly equal doses, though I suspect only the first will be taken.

You ask in your last letter how the practice fares. I answer: it fares as a cart fares on our roads, which is to say sideways, and frequently into a ditch. The thaw has turned every path between here and the mill into a black porridge that swallows boots whole — I lost one to it on Tuesday and arrived at the A— estate looking like a man who had waded through the Styx and thought better of the return trip. The old Count, who fancies himself perpetually on the verge of consumption, received me in his dressing gown and informed me, with the gravity of a man announcing his own funeral, that he had counted his pulse eleven times since breakfast and found it "irregular." It was, I assure you, perfectly regular; only his counting was not.

Then there is the matter of Agafya the miller's wife, seven months gone with child and convinced the infant will be born with a caul because a magpie crossed her path twice in one week. I have given up arguing with magpies. I only ask that she stop climbing onto the roof to fetch the wash, which strikes me as a more immediate hazard to the pregnancy than any bird's opinion of it.

As for my own constitution, dear friend, I confess it creaks. My chest has taken up a habit of wheezing in damp weather, which in this province means it wheezes from October to May and rests only briefly in June before the rains return in July. I dose myself with the same contempt I show my patients when they refuse their medicine — that is to say, I prescribe rest and take none.

I have lately been reading a translation of a French treatise on the nervous system that a colleague sent from Petersburg, and I confess it has thrown me into a fit of professional melancholy, for it describes with great confidence mechanisms I can only guess at with my stethoscope and my thirty years of watching peasants die of things with no name. There is a chapter on the influence of climate upon the temperament that I found so apt to our district that I laughed aloud and startled the cat. Send me something better, if Moscow has produced anything worth reading this winter — anything, so long as it is not about doctors, of which I have had quite enough in my waking hours.

Write and tell me of the theatre, of your dinners, of anything that does not involve a fever chart. I live vicariously through your letters, Pavel Sergeyevich, the way a starving man reads a menu. My regards to your wife, and my sympathies to your liver, which I trust you are neglecting as thoroughly as ever.

Your friend in exile among the birches and the bicarbonate,

Nikolai Andreyevich Rezvov
